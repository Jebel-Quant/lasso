\title{The Constrained LASSO is a Portfolio\\[6pt]
{\normalsize A numerical check of the CLA--LASSO identity under constraints}}
\author{Thomas Schmelzer\\[2pt]
{\small Jebel Quant Research, Abu Dhabi}\\[1pt]
{\small ORCID \href{https://orcid.org/0009-0009-9771-3501}{0009-0009-9771-3501}}}

\begin{abstract}
Schmelzer and Hastie showed that the solution path of the constrained LASSO and the
path of Markowitz's Critical Line Algorithm (CLA) are the same curve. This note checks
that identity numerically. It poses a constrained regression as a portfolio and hands
it to a released CLA written for portfolios. On caps, signs, equality and inequality
rows, monotone fits and zero sums, on simulated and real data, up to 500 predictors and
on problems built to break a path algorithm, the paths reproduce LARS, an interior-point
solver and three independent path algorithms, and meet the LASSO's optimality
conditions to rounding. A design with two nearly identical columns is declined with an
error rather than answered wrongly. With more predictors than observations the paths
stay exact, where the path algorithms for general constraints add a ridge that moves
the end of the path.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} constrained LASSO; critical line algorithm; solution path;
compositional data; portfolio optimisation; reproducibility
\end{abstract}

\section{A constrained regression}
\label{sec:example}

Tibshirani \cite{tibshirani1996} introduced the LASSO as least squares with a single
constraint, a budget on the $\ell_1$ norm of the coefficients:
\[
\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|_2^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\beta\|_1\le t,
\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad
\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_1 .
\]
LARS \cite{efron2004} and the homotopy method \cite{osborne2000} compute the solution
for every $t$, or every $\lambda$, at once. The solution is piecewise linear in the
parameter, so the whole \emph{path} is a finite list of \emph{knots}. scikit-learn's
\texttt{lars\_path} \cite{sklearn} takes $X$, $y$ and one option, \texttt{positive},
for nonnegative coefficients. There is no place for any other constraint.

Many regressions need one. The diabetes data that LARS was introduced with
\cite{efron2004} are an example: $n=442$ patients, ten standardised baseline variables
and, as the response, a measure of disease progression a year later. Six of the
variables are blood serum measurements, and three of them are closely related measures
of cholesterol: total cholesterol (\texttt{s1}), LDL (\texttt{s2}) and HDL
(\texttt{s3}). Least squares plays them off against each other. It gives total
cholesterol a coefficient of $\exTC$ and LDL one of $\exLDL$: two large effects of
opposite sign that mostly cancel. HDL, whose low levels are a risk factor for diabetes
\cite{wilson2007}, gets $\exHDL$, as if more of it made the disease progress faster. The LASSO path ends at the least-squares fit, so it gets there
too. A cap on every coefficient, $|\beta_j|\le u$, stops the offsetting; we take
$u=\exCap$, half the largest least-squares coefficient.

Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows what the cap does. Without it the path passes the cap at
\exOver{} of its \exKnots{} knots and ends with HDL positive. With it no coefficient
passes the cap, and HDL stays negative along the whole path, although no sign is
imposed. That does not hang on the choice of $u$: every whole-number cap up to
\exKeepHi{} keeps HDL negative along the whole path, and only looser caps let it turn
positive. The capped path has \exCapped{} knots.

Both panels are exact paths. They come from the Critical Line Algorithm (CLA), which
Markowitz \cite{markowitz1956} wrote for portfolios, applied to one of the two encodings
described below. The open-source package \texttt{cvxcla} \cite{cvxcla} carries them out
(Algorithm~1), and the right panel is one call:
\begin{lstlisting}
from cvxcla import Lasso
knots = Lasso(X, y, lower=-u * np.ones(p), upper=u * np.ones(p)).path
\end{lstlisting}
The same call also takes signs (a bound of zero), equality rows \texttt{a, b} and
inequality rows \texttt{g, h}, and picks the encoding that fits them.

\paragraph{What this note shows} Schmelzer and Hastie \cite{perspective} prove that
the CLA and the constrained LASSO \cite{james2020,gaines2018} trace the same curve;
Table~\ref{tab:dictionary} gives the dictionary. Portfolio managers have always needed
constraints, such as a budget, position limits and sector exposures, and Markowitz built
them into the CLA from the start. So a CLA written for portfolios, with a thin layer
that poses the regression as a portfolio, should give exact constrained-LASSO paths.
This note shows that it does, or stops with an error where its arithmetic cannot be
trusted, and checks every path against references that know nothing of portfolios.
The layer is two encodings, Propositions~\ref{prop:penalty} and~\ref{prop:budget}. The propositions need nothing from
\cite{perspective} beyond the classical equivalence of the bound and penalty forms of
the LASSO; that paper supplies the general identity of which they are instances. The
checks also exposed two defects in the CLA that refused ordinary portfolio problems too,
a tie for the largest expected return and a covariance of low rank. Both were fixed in
\texttt{cvxcla} 2.6.0 \cite{cvxcla}; the release used here is 2.7.0. Brodie et al.\ \cite{brodie2009} put an
$\ell_1$ penalty on Markowitz portfolios to make them sparse, and a companion note
\cite{lassonote} computes efficient frontiers with a LASSO solver. Both use regression
to build portfolios. This note goes the other way. Section~\ref{sec:cla} recalls the
CLA, Sections~\ref{sec:penalty} and~\ref{sec:budget} give the encodings,
Section~\ref{sec:evidence} checks the paths and Section~\ref{sec:limits} says where
they stop.

The \emph{penalty form} (Section~\ref{sec:penalty}) keeps $\lambda$ as the parameter and
takes bounds, equality rows $A\beta=b$ and inequality rows $C\beta\le h$; it is the
general form. The \emph{budget form} (Section~\ref{sec:budget}) keeps the $\ell_1$
budget, as Markowitz's long-only, fully invested problem with a cash asset, and takes
homogeneous constraints such as the zero sum $\one\trans\beta=0$ of compositional data
in one run.

\paragraph{Related work} Exact paths of the constrained LASSO are not new. Gaines, Kim
and Zhou \cite{gaines2018} derive a path algorithm from its optimality conditions that
takes general linear rows. For $C\beta=0$ alone, the Python package \texttt{c-lasso}
\cite{classo} has a path algorithm with a small ridge term added. For zero sums on
blocks of coefficients, the comlasso algorithm of Jeon et al.\ \cite{jeon2020} traces the
exact path, also with $p>n$. None was derived with portfolios in mind, so
Section~\ref{sec:evidence} runs all three as independent checks. Hobson, Butcher and
Lentati \cite{hobson2026} revisit Gaines et al.\ with CLASSO. The linear system of
Gaines et al.\ is singular whenever a sign constraint binds on a zero coefficient, so
CLASSO takes the direction from a small quadratic program instead. In the CLA a sign or a cap is a
bound, not a row, so that singularity does not arise. CLASSO adds a ridge when $n<p$,
and its code had not been released when we wrote, so we do not run it. The CLA is an
active-set method of the same kind, written for portfolios and used here as released.
With $p>n$ the other path algorithms for general constraints add a ridge, which
decides where their path ends; the CLA needs none (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Microbiome
studies often have more taxa than samples, so this is the case a user of the zero-sum
LASSO meets first.

\paragraph{Notation} The design is $X\in\R^{n\times p}$, with $n$ observations and $p$
predictors, and the response is $y\in\R^n$. Write $G=X\trans X$ and $c=X\trans y$. The
constrained LASSO is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lasso}
\mathrm{L}_\lambda:\quad
\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_1
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad A\beta=b,\quad C\beta\le h,\quad l\le\beta\le u,
\end{equation}
with $A\in\R^{m\times p}$, $C\in\R^{k\times p}$ and $l_j\le0\le u_j$, infinite
entries allowed, and a nonempty feasible set. A bound that excludes zero, such as
$\beta_j\ge\ell$ with $\ell>0$, is written as the row $-\beta_j\le-\ell$. With no rows
and infinite bounds it is Tibshirani's LASSO. We assume,
as \cite{perspective} does, that $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ has a unique minimiser
$\beta(\lambda)$ for every $\lambda>0$. Without constraints this holds when the columns
of $X$ are in general position \cite{tibshirani2013}, and with constraints when $X$ has
full column rank \cite[Theorem~6]{hobson2026}. For constrained problems with $p>n$ we
know of no such result. The checks of Section~\ref{sec:evidence} test optimality, not
uniqueness: where the minimiser is not unique, they confirm that the path is a path of
minimisers.

\section{The Critical Line Algorithm}
\label{sec:cla}

The CLA starts with the portfolio of highest expected return and lowers the risk
tolerance; between corners, the \emph{turning points} or knots, the optimal portfolio
moves along a straight line. Precisely, it solves the family of quadratic programs
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cla}
\mathrm{P}_\gamma:\quad
\min_x\ \tfrac12\,x\trans\Sig x-(q+\gamma\mu)\trans x
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Ax=b,\quad Cx\le h,\quad l\le x\le u,
\end{equation}
for every $\gamma$ from $+\infty$ down to $0$, with $\Sig\succeq0$. Markowitz's
algorithm \cite{markowitz1956} handles general linear constraints; we write them as
equality rows, inequality rows and bounds, the form \texttt{cvxcla} takes. Here $x$ is the
vector of holdings, $\Sig$ the covariance, $\mu$ the expected returns and $\gamma$ the
risk tolerance. The term $q$ is a benchmark. With $q=\Sig\bar x$ the objective is, up to
a constant, the tracking variance against a portfolio $\bar x$ less $\gamma\mu\trans x$:
the same CLA after the bounds and right-hand side are shifted by $\bar x$.
With $q=0$ it is Markowitz's problem.

\paragraph{Segments} Write
$g=\Sig x-q-\gamma\mu+A\trans\nu+C\trans\eta$ for the gradient of the Lagrangian. A point
$x$ with multipliers $\nu$ and $\eta$ is optimal when $g_j=0$ on the \emph{free}
coordinates $\F=\{j:l_j<x_j<u_j\}$, $g_j\ge0$ where $x_j=l_j$, and $g_j\le0$ where
$x_j=u_j$. In addition $\eta\ge0$, with $\eta_i=0$ on every row that is slack,
$C_ix<h_i$. Call the coordinates at a bound \emph{blocked}, $\B$, and the rows held
tight \emph{active}, $\mathcal{R}$. An active row is held as an equality. In the system
below it is stacked under $A$, with its right-hand side $h_i$ under $b$ and its
multiplier $\eta_i$ under $\nu$; the slack rows take no part. To keep the display short, $A$,
$b$ and $\nu$ stand for these stacks. With $\F$, $\B$ and $\mathcal{R}$ fixed, $x_\F$
and $\nu$ solve the bordered system
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:border}
\begin{bmatrix}\Sig_{\F\F}&A_\F\trans\\A_\F&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x_\F\\\nu\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}q_\F-\Sig_{\F\B}x_\B\\b-A_\B x_\B\end{bmatrix}
+\gamma\begin{bmatrix}\mu_\F\\0\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
so $x_\F=\alpha+\gamma\delta$ and $\nu=\nu_0+\gamma\nu_1$ are affine in $\gamma$. The
gradient on the blocked coordinates is affine too, $g_\B=g_0+\gamma g_1$. One
factorisation and two right-hand sides give the whole segment.

\paragraph{Events} Lowering $\gamma$, the segment ends at the first of four events
\cite[Section~2.1]{perspective}: a free coordinate reaches a bound, a blocked
coordinate's gradient changes sign, a slack row becomes tight, or an active row's
multiplier falls to zero. Each is the root of an affine function of $\gamma$, such as
$\gamma=-g_{0j}/g_{1j}$. The coordinate or row concerned changes sides,
\eqref{eq:border} is solved again, and the walk continues; with no event left, the last
segment runs to $\gamma=0$.

\paragraph{The start} As $\gamma\to\infty$ the linear term dominates, and the path
starts at a vertex maximising $\mu\trans x$ over the feasible set. A linear program
finds it. Suppose the maximiser is unique, and as many of its coordinates lie strictly
inside their bounds as there are equality rows and rows tight at it. Then the bordered
matrix in \eqref{eq:border} is nonsingular and the walk can begin. This is the
nondegenerate case; \texttt{cvxcla} declines a degenerate start.

\section{The penalty form}
\label{sec:penalty}

The first encoding reads the penalty $\lambda\|\beta\|_1$ as a cost: every unit of
gross position, long or short, costs $\lambda$ units of return. To make that a linear
cost, each coefficient is split into a long and a short position, both nonnegative.
The constraints on $\beta$ become constraints on the positions, unchanged in kind.

Formally, let $S$ have a column $e_j$ for every $j$ with $u_j>0$ and a column $-e_j$
for every $j$ with $l_j<0$, so that $\beta=Sz$ with $z\ge0$. Let $\bar u$ collect the
matching bounds $u_j$ and $-l_j$. A coefficient whose bounds are both zero is fixed and
can be dropped. Every $j$ then has at least one column, and $S$ has full row rank.

\begin{proposition}[Penalty as cost]
\label{prop:penalty}
For $\lambda>0$, $\beta(\lambda)=Sz(\lambda)$, where $z(\lambda)$ solves the CLA
instance $\mathrm{P}_\gamma$ with
\[
\Sig=S\trans GS,\quad \mu=-\one,\quad q=S\trans c,\quad
\text{rows } ASz=b,\ CSz\le h,\quad 0\le z\le\bar u,\quad \gamma=\lambda .
\]
Moreover $q$ lies in the range of $\Sig$, so the benchmark reading of
Section~\ref{sec:cla} applies: the program tracks a least-squares fit and pays
$\lambda$ for every unit of gross position.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Up to the constant $\tfrac12\|y\|^2$, the CLA objective at $\gamma=\lambda$ is
$\tfrac12\|y-XSz\|^2+\lambda\one\trans z$. For a fixed $\beta$ feasible in
\eqref{eq:lasso}, the smallest $\one\trans z$ over $z\ge0$ with $Sz=\beta$ and $z\le\bar u$
is $\|\beta\|_1$. The rows hold for every such $z$, since they constrain only $Sz=\beta$. It is attained by putting $\beta_j$ on the column of its sign, which
respects $\bar u$ because $l_j\le\beta_j\le u_j$. The minimum of the CLA program over
$z$ is therefore the minimum of $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ over $\beta$. At a minimiser no
coordinate holds both signs, since lowering both by the same amount keeps $\beta$ and
reduces the cost by $2\lambda$ times that amount. Hence $Sz(\lambda)$ minimises
$\mathrm{L}_\lambda$, and by uniqueness equals $\beta(\lambda)$. For the range claim,
$c=X\trans y$ lies in the range of $G$, say $c=Gv$. Since $S$ has full row rank,
$v=S\bar x$ for some $\bar x$, and then $q=S\trans GS\bar x=\Sig\bar x$, where $S\bar x$
is a least-squares fit.
\end{proof}

The covariance $S\trans GS$ is singular when both signs of a coefficient are allowed,
but \eqref{eq:border} is not. For $\lambda>0$ the proof keeps the two halves of a
coefficient out of $\F$ together. On the free coordinates $\Sig$ is then positive
definite on the null space of the active rows, the standing assumption of
\cite[Section~2]{perspective}. The halves come apart only at $\lambda=0$, which
rounding can move to $\gamma\approx10^{-16}$; \texttt{cvxcla} treats an event whose
parameter is round-off as one at $\gamma=0$.

\paragraph{The start} Without equality rows the starting vertex is $z=0$: all
positions are closed, because each costs more than it can earn. The first event is the
first entry, at $\gamma=\max_j|c_j|$ over the allowed signs, which is LARS's
$\lambda_{\max}$. With equality rows, or a row $C\beta\le h$ with $h_r<0$ that leaves
$z=0$ infeasible, the start is the linear program $\min\one\trans z$ over $ASz=b$,
$CSz\le h$, $0\le z\le\bar u$, the point of smallest $\ell_1$ norm on the constraint set
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}). For generic $A$ and $b\ne0$ it is a unique vertex with $m$
nonzeros. The path holds still there, $\delta=0$, until a second coefficient enters.
In general, every constrained LASSO path is constant from some $\lambda_0$ on, at a
least-squares fit over the feasible points of smallest $\ell_1$ norm, and $\lambda_0$
takes the place of $\lambda_{\max}$ \cite[Proposition~3]{perspective}.

Figure~\ref{fig:path}(a) shows the penalty form on the diabetes data \cite{efron2004},
where there are no constraints and LARS can check every knot.

\section{The budget form}
\label{sec:budget}

With $A\beta=0$ the penalty form starts degenerate (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). The
second encoding starts instead from the budget $\|\beta\|_1\le t$, whose path is that
of $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ indexed by $t=\|\beta(\lambda)\|_1$ \cite[Theorem~1]{perspective},
and divides by $t$. The split coefficients then add up to at most one, which is
Markowitz's own setting. A cash asset with no risk and no return holds the rest.

\begin{proposition}[Budget as simplex]
\label{prop:budget}
Suppose $b=0$ and $l_j,u_j\in\{0,\pm\infty\}$, so that every constraint is homogeneous.
Write $\beta=tSw$ with $w\ge0$, and add a riskless asset $w_0\ge0$ with
$\one\trans w+w_0=1$. Then for $t>0$ the bound-form LASSO is the CLA instance
\[
\Sig=\begin{bmatrix}S\trans GS&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mu=\begin{bmatrix}S\trans c\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad q=0,\quad \gamma=1/t,
\]
with rows $\one\trans w+w_0=1$ and $ASw=0$ and bounds $w,w_0\ge0$. This is
Markowitz's long-only, fully invested problem with a cash asset of zero variance and
zero return. If $\eta$ is the multiplier of the budget row and $\nu$ those of
$ASw=0$, then $\lambda=t\eta$ and the LASSO's multipliers for $A\beta=0$ are $t\nu$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
As in Proposition~\ref{prop:penalty}, $\|\beta\|_1\le t$ is $\one\trans z\le t$ for the
split $z=tw$, and $w_0$ is its slack. The constraint $ASz=0$ is unchanged by the scale
$t$, which is where homogeneity is needed. A nonzero right-hand side would become
$b/t=\gamma b$, a constraint that moves with the parameter. Substituting $z=tw$ gives the
objective $t^2(\tfrac12w\trans S\trans GSw-\gamma c\trans Sw)$ plus a constant, and
dividing by $t^2>0$ gives $\mathrm{P}_\gamma$. For the multipliers, multiply the CLA's
stationarity row for a free coordinate,
$(S\trans GSw)_j-\gamma(S\trans c)_j+\eta+(S\trans A\trans\nu)_j=0$, by $t$. This gives
$(S\trans(G\beta-c))_j+t\eta+(S\trans A\trans t\nu)_j=0$, the LASSO's stationarity
condition with penalty $t\eta$ and constraint multipliers $t\nu$. The cash row reads
$\eta\ge0$ while cash is held at zero.
\end{proof}

The two ends of the CLA path have plain meanings. At $\gamma=\infty$, $t=0$, the start
is the linear program $\max c\trans Sw$ over the simplex: put the whole budget on the
best asset. Without equality rows that is the predictor most correlated with the
response. It enters with the sign of its correlation and with
$\lambda=\lim\eta/\gamma=\max_j|c_j|$, just as in LARS. Under $\one\trans\beta=0$ it is
one long and one short position of weight $\tfrac12$ each, the most and the least
correlated predictors, entering together. That is why two coefficients leave zero at
once in Figure~\ref{fig:real}. Both starts are generically unique and
nondegenerate.

At the other end, cash leaves its bound when its gradient $\eta$ reaches zero: then
$\lambda=0$ and the LASSO path is over (Figure~\ref{fig:portfolio}(b)). The run stops
there. That event wins a tie, which arises when $p>n$ because every correlation
vanishes at $\lambda=0$ together with $\eta$. The path then ends at the limit
$\lambda\to0^+$, where the fit first interpolates \cite[Proposition~3]{perspective}.

The two forms give the same knots unless the support's sign vector lies in the span of
the active constraint normals \cite[Corollary~1 and Proposition~1]{perspective}. Under a
zero sum that never happens: a nonzero $\beta$ that sums to zero has coefficients of
both signs.

\medskip\noindent
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}
\textbf{Algorithm 1: the constrained LASSO path from a CLA.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Form $G=X\trans X$, $c=X\trans y$ and the split $S$ of Section~\ref{sec:penalty}.
\item If there are equality rows and every constraint is homogeneous ($b=0$,
$l_j,u_j\in\{0,\pm\infty\}$, no inequality rows), use the budget form of
Proposition~\ref{prop:budget}. Run the CLA from $\gamma=\infty$ and stop when cash
leaves zero. A turning point at $\gamma$ with budget multiplier $\eta$ is the knot
$\beta=Sw/\gamma$ at $\lambda=\eta/\gamma$.
\item Otherwise use the penalty form of Proposition~\ref{prop:penalty} and run the CLA
from $\gamma=\infty$ to $0$. A turning point at $\gamma$ is the knot $\beta=Sz$ at
$\lambda=\gamma$. With $b\ne0$, or a row with $h_r<0$, the start is the feasible point
of smallest $\ell_1$ norm (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\item Between two knots $\beta$ is affine in $\lambda$, so any other point on the path is
an interpolation.
\end{enumerate}
\texttt{cvxcla.Lasso(X, y, a=A, b=b, g=C, h=h, lower=l, upper=u)} (release 2.7.0)
carries out steps~1--4 and returns the knots. With no constraint at all it traces the
plain LASSO by a homotopy instead.
\end{minipage}}

\section{Numerical evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

Each check tests a prediction of \cite{perspective}. Their Lemma~1 makes
$\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ and the CLA one parametric quadratic program, so the penalty form
should return the knots of $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$: LARS tests this knot by knot where it
applies, and the interior-point solver and the optimality conditions everywhere.
Their Theorem~1 and Corollary~1 give the bound form the same knots, so the budget form
should return them too: comlasso and \texttt{c-lasso} test this on the zero sums.

\subsection{Test problems and checks}
\label{sec:checks}

Table~\ref{tab:evidence} lists the test problems and their constraints. The diabetes
problems use the data of Section~\ref{sec:example}, with the response centred, and
\emph{Box} is the opening example. \emph{Nonnegative} carries the one constraint
\texttt{lars\_path} accepts. In \emph{Cholesterol}, $h$ is half the least-squares net
effect of the three cholesterol measures, and the two rows bind at the end of the path.
\emph{Pinned} fits the first patient exactly, one nonhomogeneous equality row; its path
holds still at its starting vertex until $\lambda=\evPinnedHold$. \emph{Wide} is a
Gaussian design with 8 nonzero coefficients and noise of standard deviation $0.5$.
\emph{Zero-sum} is a regression on compositional data in the style of Lin et al.\
\cite{lin2014}, with the log-shares of $p$ parts as predictors. A composition is known
only up to scale, so only a zero sum makes the fit independent of the units. Five
parts have nonzero true coefficients. The signs of
\emph{Zero-sum, signs} fall on the even-numbered parts, which the truth meets. The caps
of \emph{Zero-sum, caps} are at half the largest zero-sum least-squares coefficient.
\emph{Monotone} is a Gaussian design with $n=100$, $p=\moP$ and an ordered truth, under
the rows $\beta_j-\beta_{j+1}\le0$. \emph{Sum to one}
predicts one unit's outcomes over 40 periods from those of 20 donors, as a synthetic
control does, with weights that may be negative.
\emph{sCD14} is the same model on real data from a study of the gut microbiome in HIV
infection \cite{noguerajulian2016}, as distributed with the R package
\texttt{coda4microbiome} \cite{calle2023}. The predictors come from the counts of
\rdGenera{} bacterial genera in \rdSamples{} stool samples, and the response is the
level of sCD14, a marker of inflammation, in thousands. Each count gets a pseudocount of one, since a zero has no
logarithm, and the predictors are the centred log-shares. Its path has \rdKnots{} knots
(Figure~\ref{fig:real}).

Every path comes from \texttt{cvxcla.Lasso} 2.7.0. The bounds are passed even where
they are infinite, so that the CLA computes it. Where LARS applies it is the reference,
compared knot by knot in both $\lambda$ and $\beta$. Elsewhere the reference is an
interior-point solve (Clarabel through CVXPY, tolerances $10^{-12}$) at the midpoint of
every segment. There the path is affine in $\lambda$, so it is known exactly from its
two ends. This comparison is only as sharp as the solver. Its largest gap in
Table~\ref{tab:evidence} is \evIpmWorst{}, relative, on a path whose optimality
conditions hold to rounding. The third check evaluates the optimality conditions of \eqref{eq:lasso}
directly at every knot and along every segment. A linear program picks the multipliers
$\kappa$ of $A\beta=b$ and $\eta\ge0$ of the active rows of $C\beta\le h$ that satisfy
the conditions best. The violation is then evaluated exactly at those multipliers, so
the solver's own tolerance cannot hide one. With
$r=G\beta-c+A\trans\kappa+C\trans\eta$ the conditions are
$r_j=-\lambda\operatorname{sign}\beta_j$ on the interior of the support;
$|r_j|\le\lambda$ at zero, or only $r_j\ge-\lambda$ under $\beta_j\ge0$ and
$r_j\le\lambda$ under $\beta_j\le0$; $r_j\le-\lambda$ at a cap $u_j>0$ and
$r_j\ge\lambda$ at a floor $l_j<0$; $\eta\ge0$; and primal feasibility. The check
covers each segment whole, not only its midpoint, as Hobson et al.\ \cite{hobson2026}
do. Which coefficients are zero, at a bound or free, and which rows are active, is
read off the midpoint. With that fixed, and the multipliers taken affine between the two ends, every
condition is affine in $\lambda$. It holds along the whole segment if it holds at both
ends. A linear program finds multipliers at both ends under the midpoint's pattern, and
both ends must fit that pattern: inside a segment no coefficient may change sign or
leave a bound, and no row may come loose. A path that is right at both ends of a
segment and at its midpoint, but cuts across a knot, fails this check. The published
tests include one. The largest violation, relative to
$\|c\|_\infty$, is the last column of the table.

\subsection{Accuracy}
\label{sec:accuracy}

The CLA returns LARS's knots to $10^{-13}$, including on the wide design, where
\evWideKnots{} knots carry the path to the interpolating fit. The KKT residuals are at
the level of rounding throughout, including on the problems LARS cannot take. In
positive mode \texttt{lars\_path} labels as $\alpha=0$ a point that is the path at
$\lambda=\evLarsEnd$, so the CLA's last knot is compared with
\texttt{scipy.optimize.nnls} \cite{lawson1974} instead.

\subsection{Scale and cost}
\label{sec:scale}

Table~\ref{tab:scale} extends the test to simulated zero-sum
problems of up to $p=500$ parts. At that size a midpoint solve per segment would cost
more than the path itself, so the table reports only the optimality conditions. They
hold to rounding. A second check at the largest size comes from the other encoding. The
penalty form, relaxed, returns the same \encLargestKnots{} knots to \encLargestGap{}, as
it does on every zero-sum problem here (\texttt{figures/encodings.tex}).
For a sense of cost, take a grid of 100 interior-point solves (Clarabel through CVXPY,
compiled once with $\lambda$ as a parameter). It costs about 100 times as much as the
whole CLA path at $p=100$, 350 times at $p=500$, and 14 to 100 times on the constrained
diabetes and zero-sum problems of Table~\ref{tab:evidence}. These are ratios of the
fastest of several runs on an Apple M4 Pro, the only numbers the script does not write

\subsection{Hostile problems}
\label{sec:hostile}

Table~\ref{tab:stress} adds problems chosen to make a path algorithm fail, each drawn with
\stSeeds{} seeds, and reports the worst case. \emph{Correlated} is a Gaussian design

\emph{Box}.
\emph{Wide, zero-sum} puts a zero sum on \emph{Wide}, where $p>n$.

Every path that is traced meets the optimality conditions to \stKKT{} or better. The
gaps to the references grow with the conditioning. Against LARS the gap stays below

zero-sum}, with $\operatorname{cond}(X)$ near $10^{16}$, is traced, with the largest
residual in the table (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Three independent path algorithms}
\label{sec:independent}

Three path algorithms derived for the LASSO, with no reference to portfolios, can be
run against the CLA. Started at the exact first knot, all three trace its path, up to
the ridge two of them add.

comlasso \cite{jeon2020} traces the exact path under zero sums on blocks of
coefficients, with no ridge and with $p>n$, so it should agree with the CLA with no
workaround. Its authors publish it as an R package built for R~3.2. We load it
unchanged (Code availability) and run it on \emph{Zero-sum}, \emph{sCD14} and
\emph{Wide, zero-sum}. It returns the CLA's knots, to \coGap{} relative to the largest
coefficient on the first two and to \coWideGap{} on \emph{Wide, zero-sum} over the
\stSeeds{} seeds.

The algorithm of Gaines et al.\ \cite{gaines2018} ships in MATLAB and in Julia. We
ported the Julia package to Julia~1 without changing the algorithm (Code availability)
and ran it on every problem of Table~\ref{tab:evidence}. Started at the exact first
knot, where that is simple to state, it agrees with the CLA to \gaStarted{} on the
\gaStartedCount{} such problems with $n\ge p$. On \emph{Wide}, where $n<p$, its ridge
keeps it \gaWide{} away. As shipped, its own start puts it off the path on all
\gaProblems{} problems, by \gaShippedMin{} to \gaShippedMax{} relative to the largest
coefficient. It takes the first knot from a linear program that does not involve $y$,
and calls a coefficient active above an absolute threshold of $10^{-4}$.

\texttt{c-lasso} \cite{classo} has a path algorithm for $C\beta=0$ alone, without bounds
or signs, so it runs on \emph{Zero-sum}, \emph{sCD14} and \emph{Wide, zero-sum}. It
adds a ridge term $10^{-3}I$ to $X\trans X$, fixed in the package, and that term is the
whole of its distance to the CLA. On the first two its \clPoints{} grid points lie up
to \clGap{} from the CLA path as shipped, relative to the largest coefficient. With the
ridge set to $10^{-12}$ by hand they lie \clGapUnridged{} from it. With $p>n$ the same ridge is
not small. On \emph{Wide, zero-sum}, \texttt{c-lasso} as shipped lies \clWideGapLo{}
to \clWideGapHi{} times the largest coefficient from the CLA over the \stSeeds{} seeds,
and \clWideGapUnridged{} with the ridge at $10^{-12}$.


\section{Where the two forms stop}
\label{sec:limits}

The two forms stop in two places: at a degenerate start, and at a free block that is
singular to working precision.

\paragraph{Degenerate starts} With $b=0$ the penalty form's starting vertex is $z=0$,
with no free coordinates for $m>0$ rows. This is a degenerate vertex: the multipliers at
the start are not determined by the active set, and \eqref{eq:border} is singular. For
homogeneous equality rows with signs the budget form is the remedy, because rescaling to
the simplex moves the start off the origin. It cannot take rows or caps, which move with
the parameter once rescaled (Proposition~\ref{prop:budget}). Caps or inequality rows
under a zero sum therefore leave only the penalty form, which starts degenerate there. So do
homogeneous rows ($h=0$), such as the ordering constraints of a monotone fit: all are
tight at $\beta=0$.

\texttt{cvxcla} resolves both by relaxation, an implementation device rather than part
of the method. It traces the problem twice, with the homogeneous rows relaxed to
$h_r=\varepsilon$ and $2\varepsilon$ and a zero sum to $A\beta=\varepsilon Ad$ and
$2\varepsilon Ad$ for a generic feasible point $d$. Both runs start nondegenerate. The relaxation enters only the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:border}, so where the two runs pass through the same active sets their knots
are affine in $\varepsilon$, and
$2(\lambda_k,\beta_k)(\varepsilon)-(\lambda_k,\beta_k)(2\varepsilon)$ is the knot at
$\varepsilon=0$. That condition cannot be checked in advance. \texttt{cvxcla} therefore
returns the extrapolated path only if a linear program finds multipliers that satisfy
the optimality conditions of \eqref{eq:lasso} at every knot and segment midpoint, to
$10^{-9}$ relative to $\|c\|_\infty$. Otherwise it retries with smaller $\varepsilon$
and finally declines. The paths it returns are then checked like every other path in
Section~\ref{sec:evidence} (\emph{Monotone} and \emph{Zero-sum, caps},
Table~\ref{tab:evidence}).

\paragraph{Starts that need no relaxation} Rows $C\beta\le h$ with $h>0$ enter the
penalty form as rows $CSz\le h$ of the CLA. They are slack at the start $z=0$, so the
start is unchanged. A row joins the active set when the path reaches it, like a
bound. \emph{Cholesterol} tests this (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}).

Other starts are nondegenerate but lie away from $\beta=0$. A nonhomogeneous row can
make the starting linear program non-unique. The standard case is the fully invested
constraint $\one\trans\beta=1$ of sparse portfolio regression \cite{brodie2009}: every
long-only $\beta$ in the simplex has $\|\beta\|_1=1$. The path then starts at the
least-squares fit over the simplex and stays there \cite[Proposition~3]{perspective}.
Finding it takes a quadratic program rather than a linear one, and \texttt{cvxcla}
starts on the tied face where its quadratic term is smallest, which is that fit
(\emph{Sum to one}, Table~\ref{tab:evidence}). A row with $h_r<0$ leaves $\beta=0$
infeasible, and the penalty form starts at the feasible point of smallest $\ell_1$
norm, as for \emph{Pinned}. \emph{Floor} tests this. The diabetes LASSO with
$\beta_{\texttt{bmi}}$ at least half its least-squares value has \flKnots{} knots. It
lies within \flGap{} of an interior-point solve at every midpoint and meets the
optimality conditions to \flKKT{}.

\paragraph{More predictors than observations} With equality rows and $p>n$, $X\trans X$
is singular. The bordered system, however, needs a positive definite Hessian only on the
null space of the active rows, the standing assumption of \cite{perspective}, so the
free set can outgrow the rank of $X$. As $\lambda\to0^+$ the path ends at the
least-squares fit of smallest $\ell_1$ norm under the constraints
\cite[Proposition~3]{perspective}, here an interpolating fit. \texttt{cvxcla} stops
there (Tables~\ref{tab:stress} and~\ref{tab:wide}). The other path algorithms for general constraints add a ridge:
$10^{-4}$ in Gaines et al.\ when $n<p$, $10^{-3}$ in \texttt{c-lasso}, and
$10^{-4}\max_j G_{jj}$ in CLASSO. At that ridge, the authors of CLASSO report
objectives up to $4\times10^{-2}$, relative, above the unregularised ones
\cite[Table~3]{hobson2026}.
Each then traces a nearby problem whose path ends elsewhere.

\paragraph{What stays out of reach} When two columns nearly coincide and both become
free, moving weight from one to the other barely changes the fit. Unless an active row
forbids that move, \eqref{eq:border} is then singular to working precision and
\texttt{cvxcla} stops (\emph{Duplicate} at \stEpsLo{}, Table~\ref{tab:stress}). This is
the general-position assumption of Section~\ref{sec:example}, failing in floating point.
Dropping or merging one of the two columns before tracing avoids it. A relaxation can
also fail its certificate, when knots of the exact path lie closer together than the
relaxation moves them; here none was declined. Finally, we do not prove that the walk
terminates. The non-terminating examples of Hobson et al.\ \cite{hobson2026} need
multipliers that are not unique, which a nonsingular \eqref{eq:border} rules out, and
every walk here ended.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The checks here support the identity of Schmelzer and Hastie \cite{perspective}
wherever the encodings reach. On every problem, a CLA written for portfolios returns
the exact knots, or stops with an error where its arithmetic cannot be trusted. With
$p>n$ the paths stay exact with no ridge, where the other path algorithms for general
constraints trace a ridged problem that ends elsewhere.

\paragraph{When to use it} \texttt{cvxcla.Lasso} is for LASSO paths under constraints
\texttt{lars\_path} does not take: caps, signs on some coefficients, equality and
inequality rows and zero sums, also with $p>n$. It returns the knots at less cost than
a grid of interior-point solves (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). For the plain or positive
LASSO, \texttt{lars\_path} is enough. Two nearly identical columns should be merged
first.

\section*{Code and data availability}

The software is in \texttt{preprints/constrained\_lasso/} of
\url{https://github.com/Jebel-Quant/lasso}, under the MIT licence. Each script has a
lockfile that fixes Python 3.12 and every package by version and hash.
\texttt{python/make\_figures.py} computes every path with \texttt{cvxcla.Lasso} 2.7.0
\cite{cvxcla}, runs every check, and writes every figure, table and number the text
quotes. \texttt{python/classo\_check.py} runs \texttt{c-lasso} 1.0.11.
\texttt{R/comlasso\_check.R} loads comlasso 0.2 unchanged from its authors' repository
at a pinned, checksummed commit, since R~4 will not install a package built for R~3.
\texttt{julia/classopath.jl} ports \texttt{ConstrainedLasso.jl}, written for Julia~0.6,
to Julia~1.12, with each departure marked; none changes the algorithm.
\texttt{julia/gaines\_check.jl} runs it in a pinned environment. The sCD14 data are not
redistributed: \texttt{python/fetch\_scd14.py} downloads \texttt{coda4microbiome} 0.2.4
from CRAN and checks its SHA-256. \texttt{make figures} runs all five, in CI as well.

The lockfiles fix the software, not the hardware: residuals near rounding differ in the
last digits on another CPU, so the tests use tolerances between $10^{-12}$ and
$10^{-10}$, and costs are given only as ratios.
