{\normalsize A numerical check of the CLA--LASSO identity under constraints}}
\author{Thomas Schmelzer\\[2pt]
{\small Jebel Quant Research, Abu Dhabi}\\[1pt]
{\small ORCID \href{https://orcid.org/0009-0009-9771-3501}{0009-0009-9771-3501}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Schmelzer and Hastie showed that the solution path of the constrained LASSO and the
path of Markowitz's Critical Line Algorithm (CLA) are the same curve. This note checks
that identity numerically. It poses a constrained regression as a portfolio, in a
penalty form or a budget form, and hands it to a released CLA written for portfolios.
On caps, signs, equality and inequality rows, monotone fits and zero sums, on simulated
and real data, up to 500 predictors and on problems built to break a path algorithm,
the paths reproduce LARS and an interior-point solver and meet the LASSO's optimality
conditions to rounding, or the CLA stops with an error. Only rows that exclude $\beta=0$
stay out of reach. The checks also exposed two defects in the CLA itself, now fixed,
that refused ordinary portfolio problems.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} constrained LASSO; critical line algorithm; solution path; least angle
regression; compositional data; portfolio optimisation; reproducibility
\end{abstract}

\section{A constrained regression}
\label{sec:example}

Tibshirani \cite{tibshirani1996} introduced the LASSO as least squares with a single
constraint, a budget on the $\ell_1$ norm of the coefficients:
\[
\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|_2^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\beta\|_1\le t,
\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad
\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_1 .
\]
LARS \cite{efron2004} and the homotopy method \cite{osborne2000} compute the solution
for every $t$, or every $\lambda$, at once. The solution is piecewise linear in the
parameter, so the whole \emph{path} is a finite list of \emph{knots}. scikit-learn's
\texttt{lars\_path} \cite{sklearn} takes $X$, $y$ and one option, \texttt{positive},
for nonnegative coefficients. There is no place for any other constraint.

Many regressions need one. The diabetes data that LARS was introduced with
\cite{efron2004} are an example: $n=442$ patients, ten standardised baseline variables
and, as the response, a measure of disease progression a year later. Six of the
variables are blood serum measurements, and three of them are closely related measures
of cholesterol: total cholesterol (\texttt{s1}), LDL (\texttt{s2}) and HDL
(\texttt{s3}). Least squares plays them off against each other. It gives total
cholesterol a coefficient of $\exTC$ and LDL one of $\exLDL$, two large effects of
opposite sign that mostly cancel, and it gives HDL, whose low levels are a risk factor
for diabetes \cite{wilson2007}, a coefficient of $\exHDL$, as if more of it made the
disease progress faster. The LASSO path ends at the least-squares fit, so it gets there
too. A cap on every coefficient, $|\beta_j|\le u$, stops the offsetting; we take
$u=\exCap$, half the largest least-squares coefficient.

Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows what the cap does. Without it the path passes the cap at
\exOver{} of its \exKnots{} knots and ends with HDL positive. With it no coefficient
passes the cap, and HDL stays negative along the whole path, although no sign is
imposed. That does not hang on the choice of $u$: every whole-number cap up to
\exKeepHi{} keeps HDL negative along the whole path, and only looser caps let it turn
positive. The capped path has \exCapped{} knots.

Both panels are exact paths. They come from the Critical Line Algorithm (CLA), which
Markowitz \cite{markowitz1956} wrote for portfolios, applied to one of the two encodings
described below. The open-source package \texttt{cvxcla} \cite{cvxcla} carries them out
(Algorithm~1), and the right panel is one call:
\begin{lstlisting}
from cvxcla import Lasso
knots = Lasso(X, y, lower=-u * np.ones(p), upper=u * np.ones(p)).path
\end{lstlisting}
The same call takes signs (a bound of zero), equality rows \texttt{a, b} and inequality
rows \texttt{g, h}, in combination, and picks the encoding that takes them.

\paragraph{What this note checks} Schmelzer and Hastie \cite{perspective} prove that
the CLA and the constrained LASSO \cite{james2020,gaines2018} trace the same curve.
Table~\ref{tab:dictionary} gives the dictionary: the coefficients are positions,
$X\trans X$ is the covariance of the assets, $X\trans y$ is their expected return and
$\lambda$ is a risk tolerance. Portfolio managers have always needed constraints, such
as a budget, position limits and sector exposures, and Markowitz built them into the
CLA from the start. If the identity holds, a CLA written and tested for portfolios
should return the knots of a constrained LASSO, constraints included, with no change to
its code. This note checks that prediction. It was not quite met: the checks exposed
two defects in the CLA that also refuse ordinary portfolio problems, and they were
fixed in \texttt{cvxcla} 2.6.0, the release used here (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). The two encodings,
Propositions~\ref{prop:penalty} and~\ref{prop:budget}, are proved here from the
definition of the CLA. They need nothing from \cite{perspective} beyond the classical
equivalence of the bound and penalty forms of the LASSO; that paper supplies the
general identity of which they are instances. Brodie et al.\ \cite{brodie2009} put an
$\ell_1$ penalty on Markowitz portfolios to make them sparse, and a companion note
\cite{lassonote} computes efficient frontiers with a LASSO solver. Both use regression
to build portfolios. This note goes the other way.

Exact paths of the constrained LASSO are not new. Rosset and Zhu \cite{rosset2007}
characterise the regularised problems whose solution path is piecewise linear, a class
that contains it. Zhou and Lange \cite{zhou2013} trace the solution of a quadratic
program under linear equality and inequality constraints as an exact penalty on the
constraints grows. The generalised lasso of Tibshirani and Taylor \cite{tibshirani2011},
with penalty $\lambda\|D\beta\|_1$, has an exact path through its dual. Gaines, Kim and
Zhou \cite{gaines2018} derive a path algorithm for the constrained LASSO itself from
its optimality conditions. It tracks the active set and the multipliers of the linear
constraints as $\lambda$ decreases, and it takes general linear rows. The two encodings
alone start degenerate on some of them, homogeneous inequality rows and caps or rows
under a zero sum; \texttt{cvxcla} reaches those by relaxing the constraint
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}). For $C\beta=0$
alone, the Python package \texttt{c-lasso} \cite{classo} has a path algorithm with a
small ridge term added. Neither was derived with portfolios in mind, so
Section~\ref{sec:evidence} uses both as independent checks. Hobson, Butcher and Lentati
\cite{hobson2026} revisit Gaines et al.\ with an algorithm of their own, CLASSO. The
linear system of Gaines et al.\ is singular whenever a binding row vanishes on the
active coefficients, as a sign constraint on a zero coefficient does, so CLASSO takes
the direction from a small quadratic program instead. They also show that a path with
finitely many knots need not be traversed in finitely many steps: recomputing the
multipliers at every knot can step forever along one segment. Following one canonical
certificate terminates, and they check the proofs in Lean. CLASSO adds a ridge when
$n<p$, and its code had not been released when we wrote, so we do not run it. The CLA is an active-set
method of the same kind, following one family of quadratic programs; the difference is
that it was written for portfolios and is used here as released. On their own the
encodings need a nondegenerate start; where they do not have one, \texttt{cvxcla}
relaxes the constraint (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). There are two, and both are needed:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item the \emph{penalty form} (Section~\ref{sec:penalty}) keeps $\lambda$ as the
parameter, and takes coefficient bounds, general equality constraints $A\beta=b$ and
inequality rows $C\beta\le h$. It is the general form, and the one that solves the
example;
\item the \emph{budget form} (Section~\ref{sec:budget}) keeps the $\ell_1$ budget, as
Markowitz's long-only, fully invested problem with a cash asset. It takes homogeneous
constraints such as the zero sum $\one\trans\beta=0$ of a regression on compositional
data directly, in one run. The penalty form reaches a zero sum only by relaxing it,
which fails on the largest problem here (Table~\ref{tab:encodings}).
\end{enumerate}
Each is a few lines on top of a released CLA. Readers who know the CLA can skip
Section~\ref{sec:cla}.

\paragraph{Notation} The design is $X\in\R^{n\times p}$, with $n$ observations and $p$
predictors, and the response is $y\in\R^n$. Write $G=X\trans X$ and $c=X\trans y$. The
constrained LASSO is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lasso}
\mathrm{L}_\lambda:\quad
\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_1
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad A\beta=b,\quad C\beta\le h,\quad l\le\beta\le u,
\end{equation}
with $A\in\R^{m\times p}$, $C\in\R^{k\times p}$, $h\ge0$ and $l_j\le0\le u_j$, infinite
entries allowed. With no rows and infinite bounds it is Tibshirani's LASSO. We assume,
as \cite{perspective} does, that $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ has a unique minimiser
$\beta(\lambda)$ for every $\lambda>0$. This is the generic case: for the LASSO without
constraints it holds whenever the columns of $X$ are in general position
\cite{tibshirani2013}, for any $n$ and $p$.

\section{The Critical Line Algorithm}
\label{sec:cla}

In words, the CLA starts with the riskiest portfolio, the one with the highest expected
return, and lowers the risk tolerance step by step. Between steps the optimal
portfolio moves along a straight line. A step ends when a position hits a limit or an
asset at a limit wants to come off it. These corners are the \emph{turning points};
the LASSO calls them knots.

Precisely, the CLA solves the family of quadratic programs
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cla}
\mathrm{P}_\gamma:\quad
\min_x\ \tfrac12\,x\trans\Sig x-(q+\gamma\mu)\trans x
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Ax=b,\quad l\le x\le u,
\end{equation}
for every $\gamma$ from $+\infty$ down to $0$, with $\Sig\succeq0$. Here $x$ is the
vector of holdings, $\Sig$ the covariance, $\mu$ the expected returns and $\gamma$ the
risk tolerance. The term $q$ is a benchmark: with $q=\Sig\bar x$ the objective is, up
to a constant, the tracking variance against a portfolio $\bar x$ less $\gamma\mu\trans x$,
which is the same CLA after the shift $x-\bar x$ of the bounds and the right-hand side.
With $q=0$ it is Markowitz's problem.

\paragraph{Segments} Write
$g=\Sig x-q-\gamma\mu+A\trans\nu$ for the gradient of the Lagrangian. A point $x$ with
multipliers $\nu$ is optimal when $g_j=0$ on the \emph{free} coordinates
$\F=\{j:l_j<x_j<u_j\}$, $g_j\ge0$ where $x_j=l_j$, and $g_j\le0$ where $x_j=u_j$. Call
the coordinates at a bound \emph{blocked}, $\B$. With $\F$ and $\B$ fixed, $x_\F$ and
$\nu$ solve the bordered system
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:border}
\begin{bmatrix}\Sig_{\F\F}&A_\F\trans\\A_\F&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x_\F\\\nu\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}q_\F-\Sig_{\F\B}x_\B\\b-A_\B x_\B\end{bmatrix}
+\gamma\begin{bmatrix}\mu_\F\\0\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
so $x_\F=\alpha+\gamma\delta$ and $\nu=\nu_0+\gamma\nu_1$ are affine in $\gamma$. The
gradient on the blocked coordinates is affine too, $g_\B=g_0+\gamma g_1$. One
factorisation and two right-hand sides give the whole segment.

\paragraph{Events} Lowering $\gamma$, the segment ends at the largest $\gamma$ below the
current one at which either a free coordinate reaches a bound,
$\gamma=(l_i-\alpha_i)/\delta_i$ for $\delta_i>0$ or $(u_i-\alpha_i)/\delta_i$ for
$\delta_i<0$, or a blocked coordinate's gradient changes sign,
$\gamma=-g_{0j}/g_{1j}$ for $g_{1j}>0$ at a lower bound or $g_{1j}<0$ at an upper one.
The coordinate concerned changes sides, \eqref{eq:border} is solved again, and the walk
continues. If there is no event the last segment runs to $\gamma=0$.

\paragraph{The start} As $\gamma\to\infty$ the linear term dominates, and the path
starts at a vertex maximising $\mu\trans x$ over the feasible set. A linear program
finds it. If the maximiser is unique and has exactly $m$ coordinates strictly inside
their bounds, the bordered matrix in \eqref{eq:border} is nonsingular and the walk can
begin. This is the nondegenerate case. \texttt{cvxcla} declines a degenerate start.

\section{The penalty form}
\label{sec:penalty}

The first encoding reads the penalty $\lambda\|\beta\|_1$ as a cost: every unit of
gross position, long or short, costs $\lambda$ units of return. To make that a linear
cost, each coefficient is split into a long and a short position, both nonnegative.
The constraints on $\beta$ become constraints on the positions, unchanged in kind.

Formally, let $S$ have a column $e_j$ for every $j$ with $u_j>0$ and a column $-e_j$
for every $j$ with $l_j<0$, so $\beta=Sz$ with $z\ge0$, and let $\bar u$ collect the
matching bounds $u_j$ and $-l_j$. A coefficient whose bounds are both zero is fixed and
can be dropped, so every $j$ has at least one column and $S$ has full row rank.

\begin{proposition}[Penalty as cost]
\label{prop:penalty}
For $\lambda>0$, $\beta(\lambda)=Sz(\lambda)$, where $z(\lambda)$ solves the CLA
instance $\mathrm{P}_\gamma$ with
\[
\Sig=S\trans GS,\quad \mu=-\one,\quad q=S\trans c,\quad
\text{rows } ASz=b,\ CSz\le h,\quad 0\le z\le\bar u,\quad \gamma=\lambda .
\]
Moreover $q$ lies in the range of $\Sig$, so the benchmark reading of
Section~\ref{sec:cla} applies: the program tracks a least-squares fit and pays
$\lambda$ for every unit of gross position.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Up to the constant $\tfrac12\|y\|^2$, the CLA objective at $\gamma=\lambda$ is
$\tfrac12\|y-XSz\|^2+\lambda\one\trans z$. For a fixed $\beta$ feasible in
\eqref{eq:lasso}, the smallest $\one\trans z$ over $z\ge0$ with $Sz=\beta$ and $z\le\bar u$
is $\|\beta\|_1$; the rows hold for every such $z$, since they constrain $Sz=\beta$ only. It is attained by putting $\beta_j$ on the column of its sign, which
respects $\bar u$ because $l_j\le\beta_j\le u_j$. The minimum of the CLA program over
$z$ is therefore the minimum of $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ over $\beta$. At a minimiser no
coordinate holds both signs, since lowering both by the same amount keeps $\beta$ and
reduces the cost by $2\lambda$ times that amount. Hence $Sz(\lambda)$ minimises
$\mathrm{L}_\lambda$, and by uniqueness equals $\beta(\lambda)$. For the range claim,
$c=X\trans y$ lies in the range of $G$, say $c=Gv$. Since $S$ has full row rank,
$v=S\bar x$ for some $\bar x$, and then $q=S\trans GS\bar x=\Sig\bar x$, where $S\bar x$
is a least-squares fit.
\end{proof}

In code, on top of \texttt{cvxcla}'s \texttt{CLA}, which implements
Section~\ref{sec:cla}, and with both signs allowed for every coefficient:
\begin{lstlisting}
S = np.hstack([np.eye(p), -np.eye(p)])        # beta = S z with z >= 0
run = CLA(mean=-np.ones(2 * p), covariance=S.T @ G @ S, a=A @ S, b=b, g=C @ S, h=h,
          lower_bounds=np.zeros(2 * p), upper_bounds=cap, q=S.T @ c)
knots = [(t.lamb, S @ t.weights) for t in run.turning_points]   # lam = gamma
\end{lstlisting}

The covariance $S\trans GS$ is singular when both signs of a coefficient are allowed,
but \eqref{eq:border} is not: for $\lambda>0$ the proof keeps the two halves of a
coefficient out of $\F$ together, and the columns of $X$ on the support are independent
in general position \cite{perspective}. The halves come apart only at $\lambda=0$, which
rounding can move to $\gamma\approx10^{-16}$; \texttt{cvxcla} treats an event whose
parameter is round-off as one at $\gamma=0$.

\paragraph{The start} Without equality rows the starting vertex is $z=0$: all
positions are closed, because each costs more than it can earn. The first event is the
first entry, at $\gamma=\max_j|c_j|$ over the allowed signs, which is LARS's
$\lambda_{\max}$. With equality rows the start is the linear program $\min\one\trans z$
over $ASz=b$, $0\le z\le\bar u$, the point of smallest $\ell_1$ norm on the constraint
set. For generic $A$ and $b\ne0$ it is a unique vertex with $m$ nonzeros. The path then
holds still on its first segment, $\delta=0$, while the multipliers absorb the growing
penalty, until a second coefficient enters.

where there are no constraints and LARS can check every knot.

\section{The budget form}
\label{sec:budget}

The penalty form cannot take a zero sum: with $A\beta=0$ its start is degenerate
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}). The second encoding starts instead from Tibshirani's
original statement, the budget $\|\beta\|_1\le t$, and divides by $t$. The split
coefficients then add up to at most one, which is Markowitz's own setting: a long-only
portfolio that invests a budget of one. A cash asset, with no risk and no return, holds
whatever part of the budget the regression does not use.

The bound form has the same path as $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$, indexed by
$t=\|\beta(\lambda)\|_1$ instead of $\lambda$ \cite{perspective}.

\begin{proposition}[Budget as simplex]
\label{prop:budget}
Suppose $b=0$ and $l_j,u_j\in\{0,\pm\infty\}$, so that every constraint is homogeneous.
Write $\beta=tSw$ with $w\ge0$, and add a riskless asset $w_0\ge0$ with
$\one\trans w+w_0=1$. Then for $t>0$ the bound-form LASSO is the CLA instance
\[
\Sig=\begin{bmatrix}S\trans GS&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mu=\begin{bmatrix}S\trans c\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad q=0,\quad \gamma=1/t,
\]
with rows $\one\trans w+w_0=1$ and $ASw=0$ and bounds $w,w_0\ge0$. This is
Markowitz's long-only, fully invested problem with a cash asset of zero variance and
zero return. If $\eta$ is the multiplier of the budget row and $\nu$ those of
$ASw=0$, then $\lambda=t\eta$ and the LASSO's multipliers for $A\beta=0$ are $t\nu$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
As in Proposition~\ref{prop:penalty}, $\|\beta\|_1\le t$ is $\one\trans z\le t$ for the
split $z=tw$, and $w_0$ is its slack. The constraint $ASz=0$ is unchanged by the scale
$t$, which is where homogeneity is needed. A nonzero right-hand side would become
$b/t=\gamma b$, a constraint that moves with the parameter. Substituting $z=tw$ gives the
objective $t^2(\tfrac12w\trans S\trans GSw-\gamma c\trans Sw)$ plus a constant, and
dividing by $t^2>0$ gives $\mathrm{P}_\gamma$. For the multipliers, multiply the CLA's
stationarity row for a free coordinate,
$(S\trans GSw)_j-\gamma(S\trans c)_j+\eta+(S\trans A\trans\nu)_j=0$, by $t$. This gives
$(S\trans(G\beta-c))_j+t\eta+(S\trans A\trans t\nu)_j=0$, the LASSO's stationarity
condition with penalty $t\eta$ and constraint multipliers $t\nu$. The cash row reads
$\eta\ge0$ while cash is held at zero.
\end{proof}

In code, cash is one more asset with no variance and no return, and the run stops when
it enters:
\begin{lstlisting}
k = 2 * p + 1                                    # long, short, cash
Sig = np.zeros((k, k)); Sig[:-1, :-1] = S.T @ G @ S
mu = np.append(S.T @ c, 0.0)
rows = np.vstack([np.ones(k), np.hstack([A @ S, np.zeros((m, 1))])])
run = CLA(mean=mu, covariance=Sig, a=rows, b=np.append(1.0, np.zeros(m)),
          lower_bounds=np.zeros(k), upper_bounds=np.full(k, np.inf), stop=k - 1)
knots = [(t.lamb, S @ t.weights[:-1] / t.lamb)   # beta = t S w with t = 1/gamma
         for t in run.turning_points]
\end{lstlisting}

The two ends of the CLA path have plain meanings. At $\gamma=\infty$, $t=0$, the start
is the linear program $\max c\trans Sw$ over the simplex: put the whole budget on the
best asset. Without equality rows that is the predictor most correlated with the
response, entering with the sign of its correlation and with
$\lambda=\lim\eta/\gamma=\max_j|c_j|$, just as in LARS. Under $\one\trans\beta=0$ it is
one long and one short position of weight $\tfrac12$ each, the most and the least
correlated predictors, entering together. That is why two coefficients leave zero at
once in Figure~\ref{fig:real}. Both starts are generically unique and
nondegenerate.

At the other end, cash leaves its bound when its gradient $\eta$ reaches zero, so
$\lambda=0$ and the budget goes slack: the regression no longer wants a larger $\ell_1$
norm, and the LASSO path is over. Past that point the CLA still has a path, the ray
$w=\beta(0)/t$ diluted by cash, but the twin halves' gradients vanish identically along
it. The run therefore ends when cash enters, which \texttt{cvxcla}'s \texttt{stop}
does, and that event wins a tie. A tie would arise when $p>n$, where every correlation
vanishes at $\lambda=0$ together with $\eta$ and rounding alone would decide which event
the walk takes. There the path ends where the fit first interpolates, the limit
$\lambda\to0^+$, and \texttt{cvxcla} stops at it (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
Figure~\ref{fig:portfolio}(b) shows the diabetes path in these terms.

The two forms give the same knots where $t=\|\beta(\lambda)\|_1$ is strictly decreasing
in $\lambda$ \cite[Theorem~1 and Corollary~1]{perspective}, which fails only if the support's sign
vector lies in the span of the active constraint normals. Under $\one\trans\beta=0$ that
would need every nonzero coefficient to have the same sign, impossible for a nonzero
$\beta$ that sums to zero.

\medskip\noindent
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}
\textbf{Algorithm 1: the constrained LASSO path from a CLA.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Form $G=X\trans X$, $c=X\trans y$ and the split $S$ of Section~\ref{sec:penalty}.
\item If there are equality rows and every constraint is homogeneous ($b=0$,
$l_j,u_j\in\{0,\pm\infty\}$, no inequality rows), use the budget form of
Proposition~\ref{prop:budget}. Run the CLA from $\gamma=\infty$ and stop when cash
leaves zero. A turning point at $\gamma$ with budget multiplier $\eta$ is the knot
$\beta=Sw/\gamma$ at $\lambda=\eta/\gamma$.
\item Otherwise use the penalty form of Proposition~\ref{prop:penalty} and run the CLA
from $\gamma=\infty$ to $0$. A turning point at $\gamma$ is the knot $\beta=Sz$ at
$\lambda=\gamma$. With $b\ne0$ the start must be a unique vertex
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\item Between two knots $\beta$ is affine in $\lambda$, so any other point on the path is
an interpolation.
\end{enumerate}
\texttt{cvxcla.Lasso(X, y, a=A, b=b, g=C, h=h, lower=l, upper=u)} (release 2.6.0)
carries out steps~1--4 and returns the knots. With no constraint at all it traces the
plain LASSO by a homotopy instead.
\end{minipage}}
\medskip

\section{Numerical evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

The checks test predictions of \cite{perspective}. Section~2 and Lemma~1 there cast
$\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ and the CLA as instances of one parametric quadratic program, so
the penalty form should return the knots of $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$. Theorem~1 and
Corollary~1 there say that the capped program, which the budget form solves, has the
same knots wherever $\|\beta(\lambda)\|_1$ is strictly decreasing, so the budget form
should return them too. The author of \texttt{cvxcla} wrote the checks, so they are not
independent in the usual sense. The references are: LARS, an interior-point solver,
the optimality conditions evaluated without either encoding, and two path algorithms
written by others. The scripts and their tolerances are published.

Table~\ref{tab:evidence} lists the test problems. The diabetes problems use the data
of Section~\ref{sec:example}, with the response centred. \emph{Nonnegative} requires
every coefficient to be at least zero, the one constraint \texttt{lars\_path} accepts.
\emph{Wide} is a Gaussian design with $n=40$ and $p=100$, with 8 nonzero coefficients
and noise of standard deviation $0.5$. \emph{Box} is the opening example. At
$\lambda=0$ the variables \texttt{bmi} and \texttt{s5} sit at the cap, while
\texttt{s1}, whose least-squares coefficient is the largest, ends well inside it.
\emph{Cholesterol} caps instead the net effect of the three cholesterol measures,
$|\beta_{\texttt{s1}}+\beta_{\texttt{s2}}+\beta_{\texttt{s3}}|\le h$, two inequality
rows with $h$ half the least-squares value; the rows bind at the end of the path.
\emph{Pinned} requires the fitted value for the first patient to equal the observed
one, one nonhomogeneous equality row. Its starting vertex puts all weight on
\texttt{bmi}, the largest entry of that patient's covariate vector, and holds still
until $\lambda=4053$. \emph{Zero-sum} is a regression on compositional data in the
style of Lin et al.\ \cite{lin2014}. Each of $n=60$ samples is a composition of $p=30$
parts, the predictors are the logs of the shares, and the coefficients must sum to
zero: a composition is only known up to scale, and only a zero sum makes the fit
independent of the units. Five parts have nonzero true coefficients. \emph{Zero-sum,
signs} adds nonnegativity on the even-numbered parts, which the truth meets, and
\emph{Zero-sum, caps} a cap at half the largest zero-sum least-squares coefficient.
\emph{Monotone} is the ordered fit of Section~\ref{sec:limits}, with rows
$\beta_j\le\beta_{j+1}$. \emph{Sum to one} predicts one unit's outcomes over 40
periods from those of 20 donors, as a synthetic control does, with weights that must sum
to one and may be negative.
\emph{sCD14} is the same model on real data, from a study of the gut microbiome in HIV
infection \cite{noguerajulian2016}, as distributed with the R package
\texttt{coda4microbiome} \cite{calle2023}: the counts of \rdGenera{} bacterial genera
in \rdSamples{} stool samples, and as the response the level of sCD14, a marker of
inflammation, in thousands. A count of zero has no logarithm, so each count gets a
pseudocount of one, a common choice for such data; the predictors are then the logs of
the shares, centred. Its path has \rdKnots{} knots (Figure~\ref{fig:real}).

Every path comes from \texttt{cvxcla.Lasso} 2.6.0, with the bounds passed even where
they are infinite, so that the CLA computes it. Where LARS applies it is the reference,
compared knot by knot in both $\lambda$ and $\beta$. Elsewhere the reference is an
interior-point solve (Clarabel through CVXPY, tolerances $10^{-12}$) at the midpoint of
every segment, where the path is affine in $\lambda$ and so known exactly from its two
ends. This comparison is only as sharp as the solver, which reaches about $10^{-8}$
relative. The third check evaluates the optimality conditions of \eqref{eq:lasso}
directly at every knot and along every segment. A linear program picks the multipliers
$\kappa$ of $A\beta=b$ and $\eta\ge0$ of the active rows of $C\beta\le h$ that satisfy
the conditions best, and the violation is then evaluated exactly at those multipliers,
so the solver's own tolerance cannot hide one. With
$r=G\beta-c+A\trans\kappa+C\trans\eta$ the conditions are
$r_j=-\lambda\operatorname{sign}\beta_j$ on the interior of the support;
$|r_j|\le\lambda$ at zero, or only $r_j\ge-\lambda$ under $\beta_j\ge0$ and
$r_j\le\lambda$ under $\beta_j\le0$; $r_j\le-\lambda$ at a cap $u_j>0$ and
$r_j\ge\lambda$ at a floor $l_j<0$; $\eta\ge0$; and primal feasibility. The check
covers each segment whole, not only its midpoint, as Hobson et al.\ \cite{hobson2026}
do. Which coefficients are zero, at a bound or free, and which rows are active, is
read off the midpoint. With that fixed, every condition is affine in $\lambda$ once the
multipliers are taken affine between the two ends, so it holds along the whole segment
if it holds at both ends. A linear program finds multipliers at both ends under the
midpoint's pattern, and both ends must fit that pattern: no coefficient may change sign
or leave a bound, and no row may come loose, inside a segment. A path that is right at
both ends of a segment and at its midpoint, but cuts across a knot, fails this check;
the published tests include one. The largest violation, relative to
$\|c\|_\infty$, is the last column of the table.

\paragraph{Scale} Table~\ref{tab:scale} extends the test to simulated zero-sum
problems of up to $p=500$ parts. At that size a midpoint solve per segment would cost
more than the path itself, so the table reports only the optimality conditions; they
hold to rounding. For a sense of cost, a grid of 100 interior-point solves (Clarabel
through CVXPY, compiled once with $\lambda$ as a parameter) costs 15 to 350 times as
much as the whole CLA path on these problems, in ratios of the fastest of several runs,
and it misses every knot between grid points.

\paragraph{Hostile problems} The problems so far are easy for any path algorithm.
Table~\ref{tab:stress} adds problems chosen to make one fail, each drawn with
\stSeeds{} seeds, and reports the worst case. \emph{Correlated} is a Gaussian design
with $n=100$ and $p=40$ in which every pair of columns has correlation \stRho{}, alone,
under a box and under a zero sum. \emph{Duplicate} adds to the diabetes data an
eleventh column, \texttt{bmi} plus noise of \stEpsHi{} or \stEpsLo{} times its spread,
alone and under the cap of \emph{Box}, so that two columns nearly coincide.
\emph{Wide, zero-sum} puts a zero sum on \emph{Wide}, where $p>n$.

Every path that is traced meets the optimality conditions to \stKKT{} or better. The
gaps to the references grow with the conditioning, and the references account for
them. Against LARS the gap stays below $\operatorname{cond}(X)^2$ times the machine
epsilon, the scale at which conditioning lets two exact solvers part, and LARS's own
KKT residual is at most \stLarsKKT{}: both paths are right. Against the interior-point
solver the gap reaches \stIpmGap{} on \emph{Duplicate}, but the solver's objective is
never lower than the path's by more than \stIpmBetter{}, relative, which is within its
tolerance. That gap is the solver's.

\texttt{cvxcla} declines one of the problems, for every seed. Without a cap,
\emph{Duplicate} at \stEpsLo{} brings both twins into the free set, where the bordered
system is singular to working precision, and \texttt{cvxcla} stops with an error.
Under the cap the duplicate enters first and reaches the cap before \texttt{bmi}
enters, so the twins are never free together, and the path is exact. \emph{Wide,
zero-sum}, where $X\trans X$ is singular, is traced, and with $\operatorname{cond}(X)$
near $10^{16}$ it has the largest residual in the table, \stKKT{}
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Two independent path algorithms} Two path algorithms derived for the LASSO,
with no reference to portfolios, can be run against the CLA. Both trace the same path.

The algorithm of Gaines et al.\ \cite{gaines2018} ships in MATLAB and in Julia. The
Julia package, \texttt{ConstrainedLasso.jl}, was written for Julia~0.6 and no longer
loads. We ported the Julia code to Julia~1 and the current \texttt{Convex.jl} without
changing the algorithm (Code availability), and ran it on every problem of

knot, where that is simple to state, it agrees with the CLA to \gaStarted{} on the
\gaStartedCount{} such problems with $n\ge p$: \emph{Plain}, \emph{Box},
\emph{Cholesterol}, \emph{Zero-sum} and \emph{sCD14}. On \emph{Nonnegative},
\emph{Zero-sum, signs}, \emph{Zero-sum, caps}, \emph{Monotone}, \emph{Pinned} and
\emph{Sum to one} the multipliers at the first knot are not unique, or the first knot
is a face rather than a vertex, so we give it no start there and make no claim. On \emph{Wide}, where $n<p$, it
adds a ridge of $10^{-4}$ to $X\trans X$ and stays \gaWide{} away. As shipped, its own
start puts it off the path on all \gaProblems{} problems, by \gaShippedMin{} to
\gaShippedMax{} relative to the largest coefficient. It takes the first knot from a
linear program that does not involve $y$, which starts \emph{Zero-sum} at
$\lambda=\gaStart$ instead of $\gaLamMax$, and it calls a coefficient active there when
an interior-point solve puts it above $10^{-4}$, an absolute threshold that on the
diabetes data, whose loss is of order $10^6$, admits \gaSpurious{} of the \gaCoeffs{}.
The MATLAB original, which we did not run, reads its first $\lambda$ off the
multipliers of that start (\texttt{lsq\_classopath.m}, lines 165--189, 242 and 253), so
its start can differ from the one measured here.

\texttt{c-lasso} \cite{classo} has a path algorithm for $C\beta=0$ alone, without bounds
or signs, so it runs on \emph{Zero-sum} and \emph{sCD14}. It adds a ridge term
$10^{-3}I$ to $X\trans X$, fixed in the package, and that term is the whole of its

shipped, relative to the largest coefficient, and \clGapUnridged{} with the ridge set
to $10^{-12}$ by hand.

Two further packages fit on a grid of $\lambda$ rather than tracing the path, so they
cannot check the knots and are not run: \texttt{zeroSum} \cite{zerosum}, for zero-sum
elastic nets in R, and \texttt{PACLasso} \cite{paclasso}, the penalized and constrained
LASSO of James et al.\ \cite{james2020}, which CRAN archived on 2~June 2025.

\section{Where the two forms stop}
\label{sec:limits}

Alone, the two forms cover bounds, equality rows with a nondegenerate start, inequality
rows with $h>0$, and homogeneous rows with signs. Where they stop, the obstacle is at
the start, in the shape of the constraint, or in the arithmetic. This section says
where, and how \texttt{cvxcla} carries on past the first two.

\paragraph{Homogeneous rows in the penalty form} With $b=0$ the starting vertex is
$z=0$, with no free coordinates for $m>0$ rows. This is a degenerate vertex: the
multipliers at the start are not determined by the active set, and \eqref{eq:border}
is singular. \texttt{cvxcla} detects this and declines to start. The budget form is
the remedy, because rescaling to the simplex moves the start off the origin.

\paragraph{More predictors than observations} With equality rows and $p>n$, $X\trans X$
is singular and the end of the path, the constrained least-squares fit, need not be
unique. Before it, the free set can outgrow the rank of $X$, and the bordered system is
then solved on the null space of the active constraints, which keep it nonsingular.
The path ends where the fit first interpolates, the limit $\lambda\to0^+$: the
interpolating fit of smallest $\ell_1$ norm under the constraints. Past it the CLA would
run on through interpolating fits of larger norm, which are not on the LASSO path, so
\texttt{cvxcla} stops there. \emph{Wide, zero-sum} (Table~\ref{tab:stress}) tests this.
The other path algorithms tested here do not give this path: Gaines et al.\ add a ridge
of $10^{-4}$ when $n<p$, and \texttt{c-lasso} always adds one of $10^{-3}$. Nor does
CLASSO \cite{hobson2026}, not run here, which adds one of $10^{-4}\max_j G_{jj}$. It is the
case a user of the zero-sum LASSO meets first, since microbiome data often have more
taxa than samples.

\paragraph{Numerically singular free blocks} The bordered system \eqref{eq:border}
needs the columns on the free set to be independent. When two columns nearly coincide
and both become free, it is singular to working precision and \texttt{cvxcla} stops
(\emph{Duplicate} at \stEpsLo{}, Table~\ref{tab:stress}). This is the general-position
assumption of Section~\ref{sec:example}, failing in floating point.

\paragraph{Flat starts} A nonhomogeneous row can make the starting linear program
non-unique. The fully invested constraint $\one\trans\beta=1$ of sparse portfolio
regression \cite{brodie2009} is the standard case: every long-only $\beta$ in the
simplex has $\|\beta\|_1=1$ and minimises the $\ell_1$ norm. The path's first segment is
then the least-squares fit over that face, constant in $\lambda$. This is the flat
segment of \cite[Proposition~2]{perspective}, and finding the point requires a
quadratic program rather than a linear one. \texttt{cvxcla} starts on the tied face
where its quadratic term is smallest, which is that fit, so the walk begins on the flat
segment. \emph{Sum to one} tests it (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}).

\paragraph{Inequality rows} Rows $C\beta\le h$ with $h>0$ enter the penalty form as rows
$CSz\le h$ of the CLA. They are slack at the start $z=0$, so the start is unchanged and
nondegenerate, and a row joins the active set when the path reaches it, like a bound.
\emph{Cholesterol} tests this (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}). Two cases put both
encodings on a degenerate start. The budget form cannot take rows or caps: rescaled by
$t$, a row $C\beta\le h$ becomes $CSw\le\gamma h$ and a cap $\beta_j\le u_j$ becomes
$w\le\gamma u_j$, right-hand sides that move with the parameter. So inequality rows or
finite bounds under a zero sum leave only the penalty form, which starts degenerate
there. And homogeneous rows ($h=0$), such as the ordering constraints of a monotone fit,
are all tight at $\beta=0$, so the start is degenerate in either form.

\texttt{cvxcla} resolves both by relaxation. It traces the problem twice, with the
homogeneous rows relaxed to $h=\varepsilon$ and $h=2\varepsilon$ (and a zero sum
relaxed to $A\beta=\varepsilon Ad$ for a generic point $d$ the constraints admit), and
both runs start nondegenerate. On a fixed sequence of active sets the solution on each
segment is affine in the relaxation, and its slope in $\lambda$ does not depend on it,
so the knots are affine in it too, and $2\,\beta_\varepsilon-\beta_{2\varepsilon}$ is the
exact path. Segments that shrink to a point are dropped. The result is certified, not
trusted: it is returned only if a linear program finds multipliers that satisfy the
optimality conditions to rounding at every knot and midpoint, and declined otherwise.
\emph{Monotone} and \emph{Zero-sum, caps} test it (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}).

The monotone fit shows why the extrapolation is exact. On a Gaussian design with
$n=100$, $p=\moP$ and an ordered truth, the rows are $\beta_j-\beta_{j+1}\le h$. For
every $\varepsilon$ from \moHMax{} down to \moHMin{} the path at $h=\varepsilon$ has the
same \moKnots{} knots, meets its own optimality conditions to \moKKT{}, and lies
$\varepsilon$ from the path at $h=0$, to two digits: the knots move linearly in
$\varepsilon$, as the extrapolation needs. Some of its segments shrink to a point at
$h=0$, where the exact path has \moExact{} knots.

One kind of row stays out of reach. A row that excludes $\beta=0$, $C\beta\le h$ with
some $h_r<0$, cannot be relaxed into a feasible start, because every encoding here
starts the path at $\beta=0$; Gaines et al.\ and CLASSO \cite{hobson2026} start from a
feasible point and take it.

\paragraph{One encoding or two} With the relaxation, the penalty form takes a zero sum
too, which raises the question whether the budget form is still needed.
Table~\ref{tab:encodings} sends every zero-sum problem of Tables~\ref{tab:evidence}
to~\ref{tab:stress} through the penalty form as well, with caps so loose they never
bind. On all but one it returns the budget form's knots to rounding. On the largest,
$p=500$ parts and over 500 knots, the extrapolated path fails its certificate and
\texttt{cvxcla} declines it, while the budget form traces it in one run
(Table~\ref{tab:scale}). The relaxation also costs a second run of the CLA. So the
penalty form is the general encoding, and the budget form is the direct one for
homogeneous constraints, and the one in which a regression is visibly a portfolio
(Figure~\ref{fig:portfolio}(b)).

\paragraph{What the checks did for the CLA} Two of the cases in this section were
defects of the CLA, not of the encodings, and both refuse ordinary portfolio problems.
A tie for the largest expected return, two assets with the same forecast, makes the
starting vertex non-unique; the CLA of \texttt{cvxcla} 2.5.0 refused it, and 2.6.0
starts on the tied face where the quadratic term is smallest, the fix the flat start
needed. A covariance of low rank, such as a factor model without specific risk, makes
the free block singular; 2.5.0 refused it, and 2.6.0 solves it on the null space of the
active constraints, the fix the case $p>n$ needed. Table~\ref{tab:portfolios} traces
both with 2.6.0 and checks every turning point against an interior-point solve of the
same Markowitz problem. Checking the identity on regressions thus made the portfolio
solver better at portfolios.

\section{Conclusion}

Schmelzer and Hastie \cite{perspective} prove that a constrained LASSO is a portfolio
problem and that its path is the path of the Critical Line Algorithm (their Lemma~1,
Theorem~1 and Corollary~1). The checks here support the identity wherever the
encodings reach. On every problem, a CLA written for portfolios either returns the
exact knots or stops with an error when its arithmetic cannot be trusted. Where an
encoding starts degenerate, \texttt{cvxcla} relaxes the constraint, extrapolates and
certifies the result, so the checks reach monotone fits, caps and rows under a zero sum
and weights that sum to one; with a singular Gram they reach zero sums with $p>n$. Only
rows that exclude $\beta=0$ stay out. The checks also made the CLA better at
portfolios, where it now takes tied forecasts and low-rank covariances. The practical
consequence is simple: \texttt{lars\_path}
cannot take even a cap on the coefficients, and a CLA that takes general linear rows
can.
