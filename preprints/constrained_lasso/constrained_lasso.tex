\title{The Constrained LASSO by the Critical Line Algorithm\\[6pt]
{\normalsize Two ways to hand a regression to a portfolio optimiser}}
\author{Thomas Schmelzer}
\date{2 October 2026}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Schmelzer and Hastie show that Markowitz's Critical Line Algorithm (CLA) and the
constrained LASSO trace the same piecewise-linear curve. A companion note used that
identity in one direction, computing efficient frontiers with a LASSO solver. This note
goes the other way: it computes LASSO paths with an unmodified CLA that knows nothing
about regression. There are two ways to pose a LASSO as a portfolio problem. In the
\emph{penalty form} every unit of gross position costs one unit of expected return, and
the CLA's risk-tolerance parameter is the penalty $\lambda$ itself. Bounds on the
coefficients become position limits, and linear equality constraints become rows of the
budget matrix. In the \emph{budget form} the $\ell_1$ ball, rescaled, is the long-only,
fully invested simplex of Markowitz's original problem, with a riskless cash asset
holding the unused budget. The parameter is the reciprocal of the $\ell_1$ bound,
homogeneous constraints such as $\one\trans\beta=0$ carry over unchanged, and cash
entering the portfolio marks the end of the LASSO path. On the diabetes data, a
$p>n$ design and compositional data, the CLA reproduces every LARS knot to $10^{-13}$
and meets the LASSO's optimality conditions to $10^{-15}$ at every knot.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Markowitz's Critical Line Algorithm \cite{markowitz1956} traces the efficient set of a
quadratic program as a parameter moves. The solution is affine in the parameter while
the active set is fixed and turns a corner when it changes. The homotopy and LARS
algorithms \cite{osborne2000,efron2004} do the same for the LASSO
\cite{tibshirani1996}. Schmelzer and Hastie \cite{perspective} prove that the two paths
are one curve: with $\Sig=X\trans X$ and $\mu=X\trans y$, the
gross-exposure-constrained mean--variance program and the constrained LASSO
\cite{james2020,gaines2018} have the same solutions, under the same linear constraints.
A companion note \cite{lassonote} put a stock LASSO solver to work on portfolio
problems.

This note runs the identity the other way. It takes the CLA as Markowitz wrote it,
for a quadratic objective over a polyhedron with one parameter in the linear term, and
feeds it LASSO problems. Nothing in the algorithm is adapted. The work is in the
encoding, and there are two encodings:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item the \emph{penalty form} (Section~\ref{sec:penalty}), whose parameter is
$\lambda$ and which takes coefficient bounds and general linear equality constraints;
\item the \emph{budget form} (Section~\ref{sec:budget}), whose parameter is the
reciprocal of the $\ell_1$ bound. It is Markowitz's long-only, fully invested problem
with a cash asset, and it takes homogeneous constraints.
\end{enumerate}
Neither reduction is deep, and the note claims no new theorem. What it adds is a
working instance. Each form is a few lines on top of a CLA of about a hundred lines,
and the numbers in Section~\ref{sec:evidence} show that together they reproduce LARS
and a conic solver to rounding error. Section~\ref{sec:limits} sets out where both
forms stop.

\paragraph{Notation} The design is $X\in\R^{n\times p}$, with $n$ observations and $p$
predictors, and the response is $y\in\R^n$. Write $G=X\trans X$ and $c=X\trans y$. The
constrained LASSO is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lasso}
\mathrm{L}_\lambda:\quad
\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_1
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad A\beta=b,\quad l\le\beta\le u,
\end{equation}
with $A\in\R^{m\times p}$ and $l_j\le0\le u_j$, infinite entries allowed. We assume, as
\cite{perspective} does, that $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ has a unique minimiser $\beta(\lambda)$
for every $\lambda>0$, which is the generic case.

\section{The Critical Line Algorithm}
\label{sec:cla}

The CLA solves the family of quadratic programs
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cla}
\mathrm{P}_\gamma:\quad
\min_x\ \tfrac12\,x\trans\Sig x-(q+\gamma\mu)\trans x
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Ax=b,\quad l\le x\le u,
\end{equation}
for every $\gamma$ from $+\infty$ down to $0$, with $\Sig\succeq0$. In portfolio terms
$x$ is the vector of holdings, $\Sig$ the covariance, $\mu$ the expected returns and
$\gamma$ the risk tolerance. The term $q$ is a benchmark. When $q=\Sig\bar x$, the
objective is $\tfrac12(x-\bar x)\trans\Sig(x-\bar x)-\gamma\mu\trans x$ up to a
constant, the tracking variance against a portfolio $\bar x$ traded off against
expected return. This is the same CLA after the change of variables $x-\bar x$, whose
only effect is to shift the bounds and the right-hand side. With $q=0$ it is
Markowitz's problem exactly.

\paragraph{Segments} Write
$g=\Sig x-q-\gamma\mu+A\trans\nu$ for the gradient of the Lagrangian. A point $x$ with
multipliers $\nu$ is optimal when $g_j=0$ on the \emph{free} coordinates
$\F=\{j:l_j<x_j<u_j\}$, $g_j\ge0$ where $x_j=l_j$, and $g_j\le0$ where $x_j=u_j$. Call
the coordinates at a bound \emph{blocked}, $\B$. With $\F$ and $\B$ fixed, $x_\F$ and
$\nu$ solve the bordered system
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:border}
\begin{bmatrix}\Sig_{\F\F}&A_\F\trans\\A_\F&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x_\F\\\nu\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}q_\F-\Sig_{\F\B}x_\B\\b-A_\B x_\B\end{bmatrix}
+\gamma\begin{bmatrix}\mu_\F\\0\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
so $x_\F=\alpha+\gamma\delta$ and $\nu=\nu_0+\gamma\nu_1$ are affine in $\gamma$. The
gradient on the blocked coordinates is affine too, $g_\B=g_0+\gamma g_1$. One
factorisation and two right-hand sides give the whole segment.

\paragraph{Events} Lowering $\gamma$, the segment ends at the largest $\gamma$ below the
current one at which either a free coordinate reaches a bound,
$\gamma=(l_i-\alpha_i)/\delta_i$ for $\delta_i>0$ or $(u_i-\alpha_i)/\delta_i$ for
$\delta_i<0$, or a blocked coordinate's gradient changes sign,
$\gamma=-g_{0j}/g_{1j}$ for $g_{1j}>0$ at a lower bound or $g_{1j}<0$ at an upper one.
The coordinate concerned changes sides, \eqref{eq:border} is solved again, and the walk
continues. If there is no event the last segment runs to $\gamma=0$. Every corner where
an event happens is a \emph{turning point}.

\paragraph{The start} As $\gamma\to\infty$ the linear term dominates, and the path
starts at a vertex maximising $\mu\trans x$ over the feasible set. A linear program
finds it. If the maximiser is unique and has exactly $m$ coordinates strictly inside
their bounds, the bordered matrix in \eqref{eq:border} is nonsingular and the walk can
begin. This is the nondegenerate case. The implementation checks the count and refuses
to start otherwise.

\section{The penalty form}
\label{sec:penalty}

Split each coefficient into the signs its bounds allow. Let $S$ have a column $e_j$ for
every $j$ with $u_j>0$ and a column $-e_j$ for every $j$ with $l_j<0$, so $\beta=Sz$ with
$z\ge0$, and let $\bar u$ collect the matching bounds $u_j$ and $-l_j$. A coefficient
whose bounds are both zero is fixed and can be dropped, so every $j$ has at least one
column and $S$ has full row rank.

\begin{proposition}[Penalty as cost]
\label{prop:penalty}
For $\lambda>0$, $\beta(\lambda)=Sz(\lambda)$, where $z(\lambda)$ solves the CLA
instance $\mathrm{P}_\gamma$ with
\[
\Sig=S\trans GS,\quad \mu=-\one,\quad q=S\trans c,\quad
\text{rows } ASz=b,\quad 0\le z\le\bar u,\quad \gamma=\lambda .
\]
Moreover $q$ lies in the range of $\Sig$, so the benchmark reading of
Section~\ref{sec:cla} applies: the program tracks a least-squares fit and pays
$\lambda$ for every unit of gross position.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Up to the constant $\tfrac12\|y\|^2$, the CLA objective at $\gamma=\lambda$ is
$\tfrac12\|y-XSz\|^2+\lambda\one\trans z$. For a fixed $\beta$ feasible in
\eqref{eq:lasso}, the smallest $\one\trans z$ over $z\ge0$ with $Sz=\beta$ and $z\le\bar u$
is $\|\beta\|_1$. It is attained by putting $\beta_j$ on the column of its sign, which
respects $\bar u$ because $l_j\le\beta_j\le u_j$. The minimum of the CLA program over
$z$ is therefore the minimum of $\mathrm{L}_\lambda$ over $\beta$. At a minimiser no
coordinate holds both signs, since lowering both by the same amount keeps $\beta$ and
reduces the cost by $2\lambda$ times that amount. Hence $Sz(\lambda)$ minimises
$\mathrm{L}_\lambda$, and by uniqueness equals $\beta(\lambda)$. For the range claim,
$c=X\trans y$ lies in the range of $G$, say $c=Gv$. Since $S$ has full row rank,
$v=S\bar x$ for some $\bar x$, and then $q=S\trans GS\bar x=\Sig\bar x$, where $S\bar x$
is a least-squares fit.
\end{proof}

The covariance $S\trans GS$ is singular whenever both signs of a coefficient are
allowed, because the two columns cancel. The bordered system \eqref{eq:border} is
nonetheless nonsingular on every segment with $\lambda>0$: the argument in the proof
keeps the two halves of a coefficient out of $\F$ together, and under the
general-position assumption of \cite{perspective} the columns of $X$ on the support are
independent. The halves come apart only at
$\lambda=0$, where the gradient of a free half's twin is $2\lambda$ and vanishes. In
floating point that zero can land at $\gamma\approx10^{-16}$ instead, so the
implementation treats event parameters below $10^{-10}$ times the first one as zero.

\paragraph{The start} Without equality rows the starting vertex is $z=0$, all
coordinates blocked and none needed free. The first event is the first entry, at
$\gamma=\max_j|c_j|$ over the allowed signs, which is LARS's $\lambda_{\max}$. With
equality rows the start is the linear program $\min\one\trans z$ over $ASz=b$,
$0\le z\le\bar u$, the point of smallest $\ell_1$ norm on the constraint set. For
generic $A$ and $b\ne0$ it is a unique vertex with $m$ nonzeros. The path then holds
still on its first segment, $\delta=0$, while the multipliers absorb the growing
penalty, until a second coefficient enters.

\section{The budget form}
\label{sec:budget}

The penalty form keeps $\lambda$ as the parameter and gives up Markowitz's budget row.
The budget form keeps the row and gives up the penalty. Its starting point is the bound
form of the LASSO, $\min\tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|^2$ over $\|\beta\|_1\le t$, which by
Lagrangian duality has the same path, indexed by $t=\|\beta(\lambda)\|_1$
\cite{perspective}.

\begin{proposition}[Budget as simplex]
\label{prop:budget}
Suppose $b=0$ and $l_j,u_j\in\{0,\pm\infty\}$, so that every constraint is homogeneous.
Write $\beta=tSw$ with $w\ge0$, and add a riskless asset $w_0\ge0$ with
$\one\trans w+w_0=1$. Then for $t>0$ the bound-form LASSO is the CLA instance
\[
\Sig=\begin{bmatrix}S\trans GS&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mu=\begin{bmatrix}S\trans c\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad q=0,\quad \gamma=1/t,
\]
with rows $\one\trans w+w_0=1$ and $ASw=0$ and bounds $w,w_0\ge0$. This is
Markowitz's long-only, fully invested problem with a cash asset of zero variance and
zero return. If $\eta$ is the multiplier of the budget row and $\nu$ those of
$ASw=0$, then $\lambda=t\eta$ and the LASSO's multipliers for $A\beta=0$ are $t\nu$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
As in Proposition~\ref{prop:penalty}, $\|\beta\|_1\le t$ is $\one\trans z\le t$ for the
split $z=tw$, and $w_0$ is its slack. The constraint $ASz=0$ is unchanged by the scale
$t$, which is where homogeneity is needed. A nonzero right-hand side would become
$b/t=\gamma b$, a constraint that moves with the parameter. Substituting $z=tw$ gives the
objective $t^2(\tfrac12w\trans S\trans GSw-\gamma c\trans Sw)$ plus a constant, and
dividing by $t^2>0$ gives $\mathrm{P}_\gamma$. For the multipliers, multiply the CLA's
stationarity row for a free coordinate,
$(S\trans GSw)_j-\gamma(S\trans c)_j+\eta+(S\trans A\trans\nu)_j=0$, by $t$. This gives
$(S\trans(G\beta-c))_j+t\eta+(S\trans A\trans t\nu)_j=0$, the LASSO's stationarity
condition with penalty $t\eta$ and constraint multipliers $t\nu$. The cash row reads
$\eta\ge0$ while cash is held at zero.
\end{proof}

The two ends of the CLA path have plain meanings. At $\gamma=\infty$, $t=0$, the start
is the linear program $\max c\trans Sw$ over the simplex. Without equality rows that is
a single asset, the predictor most correlated with the response, entering with the sign
of its correlation and with $\lambda=\lim\eta/\gamma=\max_j|c_j|$. Under $\one\trans\beta=0$
it is one long and one short position of weight $\tfrac12$ each, the most and the least
correlated predictors, entering together. Both starts are generically unique and
nondegenerate, which is what the penalty form lacks when $b=0$
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}). At the other end, cash leaves its bound when its gradient
$\eta$ reaches zero, so $\lambda=0$ and the budget goes slack: the LASSO path is over.
Past that point the CLA still has a path, the ray $w=\beta(0)/t$ diluted by cash, but
the twin halves' gradients vanish identically along it. The implementation therefore
ends the run when cash enters, and lets that event win a tie. The tie matters when
$p>n$: there every correlation vanishes at $\lambda=0$ together with $\eta$, and
rounding alone would decide which event the walk takes. Figure~\ref{fig:portfolio}
shows the diabetes path in these terms.

The budget form gives the same knots as the penalty form only where the map from
$\lambda$ to $t$ is one-to-one, which holds where $\|\beta(\lambda)\|_1$ is strictly
decreasing \cite[Proposition~1]{perspective}. That fails only if the support's sign
vector lies in the span of the active constraint normals. Under $\one\trans\beta=0$ that
would require every coefficient on the support to have the same sign, which is
impossible for a nonzero $\beta$ that sums to zero. So the zero-sum constraint is safe.

\section{Numerical evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

Table~\ref{tab:evidence} lists the test problems. The diabetes data \cite{efron2004}
have $n=442$ patients and $p=10$ standardised baseline variables, and the response is
centred. \emph{Wide} is a Gaussian design with $n=40$ and $p=100$, with 8 nonzero
coefficients and noise of standard deviation $0.5$. \emph{Box} caps every diabetes
coefficient at $|\beta_j|\le u=396.1$, half the largest least-squares coefficient. At
$\lambda=0$ the variables \texttt{bmi} and \texttt{s5} sit at the cap, and \texttt{s1}
does not. \emph{Pinned} requires the fitted value for the first patient to equal the
observed one, one nonhomogeneous equality row. Its starting vertex puts all weight on
\texttt{bmi}, the largest entry of that patient's covariate vector, and holds still until
$\lambda=4053$. \emph{Zero-sum} uses log-compositions as predictors, in the style of
Lin et al.\ \cite{lin2014}: $n=60$ samples of $p=30$ parts, with the constraint
$\one\trans\beta=0$ that makes the fit invariant to the reference part. \emph{Zero-sum,
signs} adds nonnegativity on the even-numbered parts.

Every path is checked in three ways. Where LARS applies it is the reference, compared
knot by knot in both $\lambda$ and $\beta$. Elsewhere the reference is an interior-point
solve (Clarabel through CVXPY, tolerances $10^{-12}$) at the midpoint of every segment,
where the path is affine in $\lambda$ and so known exactly from its two ends. This
comparison is only as sharp as the solver, which reaches about $10^{-8}$ relative.
Third, at every knot and every midpoint we evaluate the optimality conditions of
\eqref{eq:lasso} directly, without reference to either form. With
$r=G\beta-c+A\trans\kappa$ they are $r_j=-\lambda\operatorname{sign}\beta_j$ on the
interior of the support, $|r_j|\le\lambda$ at zero, the one-sided versions at a cap, and
primal feasibility. The largest violation, relative to $\|c\|_\infty$, is the last
column of the table.

Three observations. First, both forms return the same knots wherever both apply, and
LARS's knots to $10^{-13}$, including on the wide design, where 53 knots carry the path
to the interpolating fit. Second, the interior-point gaps are the solver's, not the
path's. The KKT residuals of the CLA's own output are at the level of rounding
throughout, including the cases with no LARS to compare against. Third, the comparison
exposed a quirk of the reference. In positive mode, the last point that
\texttt{lars\_path} returns is labelled $\alpha=0$, but every active correlation there
equals $30.65$. It is the path at $\lambda=30.65$, where the algorithm found nothing
left to enter, rather than the nonnegative least-squares fit at $\lambda=0$. Its KKT
violation at $\lambda=0$ is $3\times10^{-2}$. Both CLA forms carry on to the
nonnegative least-squares solution, and the row in Table~\ref{tab:evidence} compares
that last knot with \texttt{scipy.optimize.nnls} \cite{lawson1974}. All earlier knots
are compared with LARS. Figure~\ref{fig:constrained} shows the two constrained paths
that have no LARS reference.

\section{Where the two forms stop}
\label{sec:limits}

Between them the forms cover coefficient bounds, nonhomogeneous equality rows with a
nondegenerate start, and homogeneous equality rows with sign constraints. The gaps are
all at the start or in the shape of the constraint.

\paragraph{Homogeneous rows in the penalty form} With $b=0$ the starting vertex is
$z=0$, with no free coordinates for $m>0$ rows. This is a degenerate vertex: the
multipliers at the start are not determined by the active set, and \eqref{eq:border}
is singular. The implementation detects this and refuses to start. The budget form is
the remedy, because rescaling to the simplex moves the start off the origin.

\paragraph{Flat starts} A nonhomogeneous row can make the starting linear program
non-unique. The fully invested constraint $\one\trans\beta=1$ of sparse portfolio
regression \cite{brodie2009} is the standard case: every long-only $\beta$ in the
simplex has $\|\beta\|_1=1$ and minimises the $\ell_1$ norm. The path's first segment is
then the least-squares fit over that face, constant in $\lambda$. This is the flat
segment of \cite[Proposition~2]{perspective}, and finding the point requires a
quadratic program rather than a linear one. Markowitz's own treatment of degenerate
starts would handle it, but it is not implemented here.

\paragraph{Inequality rows} Constraints $C\beta\le h$ enter either form as slack assets
with zero variance and zero return, one per row. With $h$ in general position this
changes nothing above. Homogeneous inequalities, such as the ordering constraints of a
monotone fit, make the slack and the bound of the same coordinate active together at
many vertices. This breaks linear independence, and the walk needs the
anti-cycling machinery of the active-set literature. Neither is in the implementation
behind Table~\ref{tab:evidence}, so the note makes no numerical claim for them.

\section{Conclusion}

The identity of \cite{perspective} says that the Critical Line Algorithm and the
constrained LASSO trace one curve. This note shows the practical consequence: the CLA,
in the form Markowitz wrote it, is a working constrained-LASSO path algorithm. The
penalty form keeps $\lambda$ and uses the bounds and rows. The budget form keeps the
simplex and uses the scale invariance of homogeneous constraints. On the problems
tested, the two forms agree with each other and with LARS to rounding error, and they
meet the LASSO's optimality conditions to $10^{-15}$ everywhere along the path.

\section*{Code and data availability}

The note's directory holds \texttt{make\_figures.py}, a single Python script that
declares its own pinned dependencies, and \texttt{make\_figures.py.lock} next to it
fixes Python 3.12 and every package it pulls in, to the exact version and hash. It contains the CLA (the function \texttt{cla}),
the two forms (\texttt{penalty\_form}, \texttt{budget\_form}) and every check.
\texttt{uv run make\_figures.py} regenerates the figures and
Table~\ref{tab:evidence}, and \texttt{uv run make\_figures.py test} runs the checks as
tests. The diabetes data ship with scikit-learn, and the other problems are simulated
from fixed seeds.
