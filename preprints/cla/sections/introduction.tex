The mean--variance portfolio selection problem \citep{markowitz1952} asks for the
allocation of capital across $n$ assets that minimizes risk for a given level of
expected return; sweeping the return level produces a one-parameter family of optimal
portfolios whose risk--return image is the \emph{efficient frontier}. The Critical Line
Algorithm (CLA) of \citet{markowitz1956, markowitz1959} represents this frontier
\emph{exactly and in full}: it returns a finite list of \emph{turning points} between
which the frontier is described in closed form, so no discretization or repeated
re-optimization is required. The canonical description and a reference implementation
are due to \citet{markowitz2000}; \citet{perold1984}, \citet{stein2008}, and
\citet{hirschberger2010} develop large-scale and active-set variants,
\citet{niedermayer2010} a careful fast implementation, and \citet{markowitz2021} a recent
review extending the method to mean--semivariance. In optimization terms the CLA solves a parametric quadratic
program. \citet{wolfe1959} adapted the simplex method to quadratic programs,
\citet{best1996} gave a parametric active-set algorithm that \citet{best2010} applies to
portfolios, and \pkg{qpOASES} \citep{ferreau2014} is a general parametric QP solver.

Classical CLA implementations take the covariance as a dense matrix. For a large
universe it usually has more structure than that: multi-factor risk models, which
generalize the single-index model \citep{sharpe1963}, write it as a diagonal matrix
plus a low-rank term. The large-scale methods cited above exploit structure through
incremental factorizations, sparsity, or pivoting tied to an explicit matrix.
\pkg{cvxcla} computes the same turning points as any CLA; its contribution is an
\emph{interface} that makes the loop independent of how the covariance is stored, so
the structure becomes a property of an exchangeable backend. The link between the CLA
and the constrained LASSO is treated elsewhere
\citep{schmelzer2026perspective,schmelzer2026lasso,schmelzer2026constrained}; this paper
documents the software.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{An operator interface.} The turning-point loop reaches the covariance
through a five-member protocol (Section~\ref{sec:operators}). A diagonal-plus-low-rank
backend solves the free block through the Woodbury identity, never forms an
$n\times n$ matrix, and traces the same turning points about $30\times$ faster than the dense
backend at $n=2560$, in under $1$~MiB of working memory against $100$~MiB, on a market
whose free set grows to every asset (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}). On a rank-$20$ factor
model of the S\&P~500, where at most $55$ of $494$ assets are free at once, it traces the
same frontier about $1.4\times$ faster (Section~\ref{sec:empirical}).
\item \textbf{General linear constraints.} Box bounds combine with any linear
equalities $A w = b$ and inequalities $G w \le h$ (Section~\ref{sec:ineq}) and a
gross-exposure cap $\lVert w\rVert_1 \le c$ (Section~\ref{sec:leverage}).
\item \textbf{A tested implementation.} A least-index tie-break makes the trace
deterministic and provably finite under standard assumptions (Section~\ref{sec:bland});
every turning point is checked for feasibility as it is found, and an independent KKT
certificate in the replication script certifies eleven traces, among them the largest
timed one ($n = 2560$) and the worked example (Section~\ref{sec:exactness}); inputs
outside the supported domain are declined with an error that names the cause
(Section~\ref{sec:errors}); and one script regenerates every figure and number.
\end{enumerate}

We do \emph{not} claim the fastest solve: on small problems \pkg{qpOASES} is faster
(Section~\ref{sec:qpoases}), and large dense problems admit faster incremental
factorizations (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}), and on a factor model whose free set grows
to every asset an interior-point solver returns a grid of $100$ approximate frontier
portfolios at a similar cost, and in parallel faster (Section~\ref{sec:clarabel}). The
claim is an exact and complete frontier at no more than the cost of a grid; on the
S\&P~500, with few assets free at once, the grid costs an order of magnitude more.

The package \citep{cvxcla}\footnote{\href{\repourl}{\texttt{github.com/cvxgrp/cvxcla}}}
is built around the class \code{CLA} in \srcfile{src/cvxcla/cla.py}{src/cvxcla/cla.py},
supported by \srcfile{src/cvxcla/first.py}{first.py} (the starting portfolio),
\srcfile{src/cvxcla/operators/__init__.py}{operators/} (the covariance backends), and
\srcfile{src/cvxcla/types.py}{types.py} (the turning-point and frontier data
structures). The notation follows the code.

Sections~\ref{sec:problem}--\ref{sec:iteration} describe the algorithm,
Section~\ref{sec:operators} the operator interface, Section~\ref{sec:usage} the user interface and
Section~\ref{sec:experiment} the experiments; details and the termination proof are in the
appendices.
