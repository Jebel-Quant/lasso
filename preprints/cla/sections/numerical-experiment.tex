\section{Numerical experiment}
\label{sec:experiment}

The experiments answer three questions, in this order: whether the trace is correct
(Section~\ref{sec:exactness}); whether its speed on large problems comes from the structured
solve rather than from bookkeeping (Sections~\ref{sec:scaling}--\ref{sec:rankscaling}); and
how it compares with the alternatives, a QP swept over a grid and a parametric QP solver
(Sections~\ref{sec:clarabel} and~\ref{sec:qpoases}). A real S\&P 500 frontier closes the section
(Section~\ref{sec:empirical}); further checks are in Appendices~\ref{sec:details} and~\ref{sec:degeneracy}. Timings are
the median of eleven repetitions up to $n = 160$, where traces are short, and of three
above, the $7$-minute \pkg{qpOASES} runs at $n=2560$ included (bands span their range),
each method and size in a freshly spawned interpreter, on the machine of Computational
details. All methods run with their default threading: the dense backends call a
multithreaded BLAS, \pkg{OSQP} and \pkg{Clarabel} solve on one thread, and the points
of a grid are solved one after another. Within a run, the repetitions above $n=160$
agree to within a few percent (\pkg{qpOASES} at $n=2560$ to $24\%$). Between two
complete runs on different days the medians moved more: by up to $17\%$ for
\pkg{cvxcla}'s backends and by up to $60\%$ for the external solvers (\pkg{Clarabel}'s
grid at $n=2560$ took $1.9$~s in one run and $3.0$~s in the other). We report the
second run and give the first where the two disagree on a ratio; the ratios are
indicative to that extent, and those at small $n$ only roughly.

\subsection{Correctness: a reference solver and a KKT certificate}
\label{sec:exactness}

On $40$ real assets (Section~\ref{sec:empirical}; condition number $\approx 200$) the CLA
traces $29$ turning points. At the midpoint $\lambda$ of each of the $27$ interior
segments we re-solve \eqref{eq:qp} with \pkg{OSQP} \citep{stellato2020}, polished and
at tight tolerances, and compare with the interpolated CLA weights: the median
discrepancy is $2\times 10^{-15}$ and the maximum $6\times 10^{-8}$, solver precision
(\target{validate-exact}). Since each segment is
affine, one interior point certifies it. The step \target{validate-constraints}
validates two constrained frontiers the same way on a $30$-asset factor market: a budget plus
a neutrality row (the LP first vertex) and a budget with per-sector caps (an inequality
active at $30$ of $31$ turning points). Both match to $3\times 10^{-14}$ or better. A third
frontier is a $130/30$ book: the budget, shorts down to $-30\%$ per asset, and the
gross-exposure cap $\lVert w\rVert_1 \le 1.3$ of Section~\ref{sec:leverage}, which binds at
$32$ of $49$ turning points; it matches to $1.4\times 10^{-12}$. The reference QP models
the cap with auxiliary variables $t \ge \lvert w\rvert$, independently of the lift.
An independent implementation of the CLA agrees as well: on the long-only problems of
Section~\ref{sec:scaling} up to $n = 640$, the \proglang{R} package \pkg{CLA}
\citep{rcla} returns the same turning points as \pkg{cvxcla}, with the
maximum-return vertex counted once, each within $5\times10^{-13}$ of its counterpart
(\code{make\_figures.py r-cla}). It takes $26$~s at $n = 320$ and $12$ minutes at
$n = 640$, so it is not part of the timing comparison of Section~\ref{sec:scaling}.

A reference solver only shows that two codes agree; a KKT certificate tests the trace
itself (\target{validate-kkt}). At three interior points of every segment, and at two
values of $\lambda$ above the first turning point, the active set is read off the
interpolated weights (within $10^{-9}$ of a bound or of $h$), the multipliers are solved
from stationarity on the free coordinates, and primal feasibility, stationarity, dual
feasibility and complementary slackness are each measured. Neither the multipliers nor
the partition come from \pkg{cvxcla}, and the certificate always uses an explicit dense
$\Sig$, never the backend that traced the frontier. Where the free coordinates do not
determine the multipliers, at a vertex with no free weight, a bounded least-squares fit
asks whether sign-feasible multipliers exist. Primal feasibility is absolute, the other
conditions relative to $\max(1, \lVert\Sig w\rVert_\infty,
\lambda\lVert\mu\rVert_\infty)$. Over $2257$ points on eight traces
(Table~\ref{tab:kkt}), including the incremental and factor backends, the general
constraints and the lifted $130/30$ problem, no residual exceeds $2\times10^{-13}$ on the
machine of Computational details ($7\times10^{-13}$ on the \proglang{Linux} run reported
there). The certificate belongs to the replication script, not to the package: at run
time \pkg{cvxcla} checks each turning point for feasibility only
(Section~\ref{sec:bland}). The
test does detect errors: weights shifted by $10^{-3}$ give a residual of
$2.5\times10^{-7}$, and weights paired with the $\lambda$ of another segment
$3.3\times10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Runtime versus problem size}
\label{sec:scaling}

We trace the full frontier for $n \in \{20, 40, \dots, 2560\}$ assets, each from a
ground-truth $K = 10$ factor model, so the dense and factor backends run the same loop
on the same $\Sig$ and differ only in the free-block solve (\target{scaling}). The free
set is not small: on this market it grows to all $n$ assets, with a median of $n/2$ over
the trace. With the roughly $n$ steps observed here, a dense trace is about $n$ solves
of $O(n_\F^3)$ each, $O(n^4)$ operations, while the per-step cost of the factor backend is $O(n_\F K^2 + nK)$, so
$O(n^2 K)$ in total. At small $n$ a step's fixed overhead hides both rates, and the two backends are at
parity up to $n\approx 80$. Over the largest four sizes ($320$ to $2560$) the fitted
exponents are $\approx 2.8$ for the dense backend and $\approx 1.4$ for the factor
backend (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}), below the operation counts; we report them as
measured and do not extrapolate them. The factor backend is about $3.6\times$ faster at
$n=640$, $12\times$ at $1280$ and $32\times$ at $n=2560$ ($36\times$ in the first run),
where it traces the $2561$ turning points in about $1.2$~s against $38$~s
(Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}). This market is the favourable case for the factor backend,
since every asset is free at some point; on the S\&P~500 of Section~\ref{sec:empirical},
where at most $55$ of $494$ assets are free at once, the gain is about $1.5\times$.

The factor backend also never holds an $n\times n$ matrix. At $n=2560$ its covariance
takes $0.22$~MiB against $50$~MiB, and the trace's working memory, the
\code{tracemalloc} peak less what the finished trace retains, is $0.84$~MiB against
$100$~MiB (\proglang{Python} objects and \pkg{NumPy} buffers; BLAS workspace is not counted). The stored frontier, $n$ weights per turning
point, is $O(n^2)$, $50$~MiB at $n=2560$, for either backend.

\subsection{Where the speed comes from}
\label{sec:baseline}

To separate the structured solve from the dense linear algebra, we set
\pkg{cvxcla}'s own dense backend against its opt-in incremental one, \code{IncrementalDenseCovariance} (Section~\ref{sec:incremental}), which runs the same loop
but maintains $\Sig_{\F\F}\inv$ by rank-one updates and reports the same turning points.
A trace is then $O(n^3)$, the measured exponent $\approx 2.2$, and the incremental
backend is about $2.7\times$ faster than the dense one at $n=640$ and $6\times$ at
$n=2560$: block elimination is not the fastest dense strategy. The factor backend is
still about $1.3\times$ faster than the incremental one at $n=640$ and $5.5\times$ at
$n=2560$, so the speed comes from the structured solve. Updating the Woodbury
capacitance $W$ incrementally would add little at $K=10$, where the $O(n)$ work of each
step (event scan, products with $\Sig$, bookkeeping) dominates, and matters only at
large $K$, where the dense backend is already faster (Section~\ref{sec:rankscaling}).

\subsection{Runtime versus factor rank}
\label{sec:rankscaling}

The factor backend's advantage must fade as $K$ approaches $n$. At fixed $n = 320$ and
$K \in \{20, \dots, 320\}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:rankscaling}, \target{rank-scaling}), the dense time is
roughly flat in $K$ while Woodbury grows as
$O(n_\F K^2 + K^3)$: the factor backend is about $1.7\times$ faster at $K=20$, at parity near
$K=160$, and $\approx 1.9\times$ slower at $K = 320$. It pays off
in the regime real risk models inhabit, a few tens of factors over hundreds or thousands
of assets.

\subsection[A general-solver baseline: a QP swept over lambda]{A general-solver baseline: a QP swept over $\lambda$}
\label{sec:clarabel}

General toolkits (\pkg{Riskfolio-Lib} \citep{riskfolio}, \pkg{skfolio}
\citep{skfolio}, \pkg{fPortfolio} \citep{fportfolio}, \pkg{CVXR} \citep{cvxr}) hand a
single risk target to a general solver, so a frontier means re-solving \eqref{eq:qp} on
a grid. We make the grid as favorable as we can: the solver is set up once, each
$\lambda$ changes only the linear term, and the grid has $100$ values of $\lambda$
(\target{scaling}). On the dense $\Sig$, an \pkg{OSQP} \citep{stellato2020} grid that
warm-starts each solve from the last grows like $\approx n^{3.2}$ and is slower than
the dense trace at every size, from about $1.5\times$ at $n=20$ to about $91\times$ at
$n=640$ ($51$~s against $0.56$~s). On the real frontier of
Section~\ref{sec:empirical} it is about $200\times$ slower at tolerance $10^{-6}$, and
at $10^{-5}$ it misses points by $10^{-3}$ (\target{osqp}).

A fair comparison hands the solver the factor model as well. With $y = U\trans w$ as
$K$ extra variables, the risk $w\trans \Sig w$ of \eqref{eq:factor} becomes
$w\trans D w + y\trans \Delta y$ under the $K$ extra equalities $y = U\trans w$: a QP
with a diagonal Hessian and a sparse constraint matrix in $n + K$ variables. Each grid
point is compared with the exact frontier at the same $\lambda$, in its weights and in
what a user reads off it, its expected return and its variance.

In this form the warm-started \pkg{OSQP} grid costs $1.7\times$ to $2.4\times$ the
factor trace up to $n=160$ and $10\times$ to $27\times$ beyond, $19$~s against $1.2$~s at
$n=2560$. The ratio is not monotone in $n$ because \pkg{OSQP}'s iteration count is not:
from $51$ iterations per grid point at $n=20$ it rises to $1867$ at $n=640$, falls to
$948$ at $n=1280$ and rises again to $2166$ at $n=2560$. Its weights are within
$4\times10^{-8}$ of the trace, and return and variance within a relative
$3\times10^{-8}$. The interior-point solver \pkg{Clarabel} \citep{goulart2024}, which
cannot warm-start but needs only $8$ to $12$ iterations per point at every size, is
faster: its grid costs $1.5\times$ to $4.1\times$ the factor trace, $1.9$~s at $n=2560$
($3.0$~s, $2.4\times$, in the first run). Its accuracy is that of its tolerance. At the
default settings the weights are within $2\times10^{-3}$ of the trace and the returns
within a relative $10^{-5}$, but the variances only within a relative $4\times10^{-3}$, so
the risk read off a grid portfolio is off in the third digit. Tightening all its
tolerances to $10^{-12}$ brings weights and variances to $2\times10^{-5}$ and
$4\times10^{-5}$ at about $1.1\times$ the time. A tolerance of $10^{-14}$ gains nothing
further: the weights stay at $10^{-5}$ and the returns become less accurate
($6\times10^{-5}$), so double-precision round-off in the solver, not the tolerance,
appears to set the limit; we did not investigate it.

On real data the picture changes with the free set. On the rank-$20$ factor model of
the S\&P~500 (Section~\ref{sec:empirical}), whose free set stays below $56$ of $494$
assets, the factor trace takes $18$~ms for $89$ turning points, while the \pkg{Clarabel}
grid of $100$ points takes $0.42$~s, $24\times$ as long, with variances off by a relative
$1.5\times10^{-3}$, and the \pkg{OSQP} grid $1.7$~s, $96\times$ as long, accurate to round-off
(\code{make\_figures.py osqp}).

Two qualifications apply. The grid points are independent, so a grid parallelizes
perfectly, while the trace is sequential; spread over the $14$ cores of the machine, the
\pkg{Clarabel} grid of the synthetic market would be faster than the trace in wall-clock
time, though not on the S\&P~500. And a grid returns $100$ portfolios, the trace every
turning point and hence, by interpolation, every efficient portfolio. With large free
sets, then, an interior-point grid on the factor form costs about as much as the exact
trace; with the small free sets of real data it costs an order of magnitude more. For a
single risk target a general solver is the natural choice; for the whole frontier,
\pkg{cvxcla} returns it complete and exact, with a certificate, at no more than the cost
of a grid.

\subsection{A parametric QP solver}
\label{sec:qpoases}

The large-scale active-set codes of \citet{perold1984}, \citet{stein2008} and
\citet{hirschberger2010}, and that of \citet{niedermayer2010}, we could not run on
current toolchains. \pkg{qpOASES} \citep{ferreau2014} solves parametric QPs by an online active-set
strategy: a hot start from one QP to the next follows the homotopy between them and
changes the working set at every breakpoint on the way. Hot-started from the
maximum-return end to $\lambda = 0$, it walks the same path as the CLA. We run it
through \pkg{CasADi} \citep{andersson2019}, solving once above the first finite turning
point and hot-starting once to $\lambda = 0$ (\target{scaling}). Its time therefore
includes CasADi's interface, which we did not separate. Called directly, \pkg{qpOASES}
can report the working-set changes it passes, and its Schur-complement variant can
exploit a sparse Hessian such as the factor form of Section~\ref{sec:clarabel}; neither
was tried.

The two codes follow the same path: \pkg{qpOASES} makes exactly as many working-set
changes as \pkg{cvxcla} reports turning points ($21, 42, \dots, 2561$), and its endpoint
matches the minimum-variance portfolio to $\sim\!2\times10^{-16}$. On small problems it
is faster, by a factor of several up to
$n=80$, where \pkg{cvxcla}'s \proglang{Python} per-step overhead dominates; the two meet
near $n=200$. Beyond that \pkg{qpOASES} grows like $\approx n^{3.7}$ and takes about $1.7$~s at
$n=640$ and $420$~s at $n=2560$ ($550$~s in the first run): about $3\times$ and $11\times$
the dense backend, and $11\times$ and several hundred times the factor backend. Its local
exponent over the last doubling is about $4.8$, steeper than its earlier growth; we did
not separate \pkg{CasADi}'s share or memory effects, so the ratio at $n=2560$ rests on a
single size and is indicative only. It also returns only the endpoint, since the
turning points it passes are not exposed through the \pkg{CasADi} interface.

\subsection[Empirical example: the SP 500 frontier]{Empirical example: the S\&P 500 frontier}
\label{sec:empirical}

The experiment runs off a frozen snapshot shipped with the package:
$N = 494$ assets over $T = 1213$ trading days, 2021-07-30 to 2026-05-29, stored as
\srcfile{experiments/data/sp500_pct_returns.parquet}{sp500\_pct\_returns.parquet}
(SHA-256 \texttt{b5faa522\ldots9937e}) and produced once by
\srcfile{experiments/fetch_sp500.py}{fetch\_sp500.py}, retained for provenance only.
That script takes the index constituents listed on the day it was run, downloads their
split- and dividend-adjusted closes from \pkg{yfinance}, drops names missing more than
$5\%$ of the trading days, and forward-fills short gaps. The universe is the index at the end of the window, with survivorship bias, so we use it
only as a realistic covariance; it says nothing about investment performance. From the
full-history sample moments (condition number $\approx 1.1\times 10^{5}$) the CLA
traces the long-only frontier, $96$ turning points, in about $25$~ms. Its free set is
small, at most $55$ of the $494$ assets and $38$ at the median, so each dense step is
cheap;
a rank-$20$ factor approximation traces a
near-identical frontier ($89$ points) in about $16$~ms. The frontier spans annualized
returns of $0.14$--$0.89$ over volatilities of $0.10$--$0.87$ (\target{real-frontier}).

\paragraph{Covariance estimators as backends.}
\label{sec:estimators}
Because \pkg{cvxcla} traces the frontier of whatever covariance it is given, a shrinkage
or factor estimate is simply another backend. Most remedies for a noisy sample
covariance \citep{johnstone2001} return the diagonal-plus-low-rank form
\eqref{eq:factor}: approximate factor models \citep{chamberlain1983}, POET
\citep{fan2013poet}, eigenvalue clipping \citep{laloux1999}, or Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage
\citep{ledoitwolf2004}, $(1-\rho)S + \rho F$. On the same universe, changing only the
covariance argument, \pkg{cvxcla} traces the frontier of the sample covariance ($96$
turning points), the Ledoit--Wolf estimate ($94$) and a rank-$20$ PCA model ($89$); the
three maximum-Sharpe portfolios hold $26$--$29$ names (\target{estimators}). As the
window shrinks past $T = n$ the sample covariance loses rank while the shrinkage and
factor estimates stay well-conditioned (Section~\ref{sec:regularization}). \pkg{cvxcla}
controls only the computational error; an exact frontier of a noisy estimate is still
noisy, and ranking the estimators would take an out-of-sample study.
